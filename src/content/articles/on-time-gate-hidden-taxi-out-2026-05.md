headline: "On time to the gate, not to the sky: at O'Hare in May, a third of punctual departures still taxied 30-plus minutes before takeoff"
subhed: "The federal on-time clock stops the moment a plane leaves the gate — the runway queue after that is invisible to it. Reading all 611,735 May 2026 flights: nationwide, 9.0% of departures that pushed back on time or early were not airborne for at least 30 more minutes. At Chicago O'Hare it was 33.3%; at St. Louis, 1.5%."

facts: |
  The U.S. Department of Transportation's on-time database for May 2026 — the most recent month posted (file in the "1987–present" reporting-carrier table) — records 611,735 scheduled domestic flights operated by 13 reporting carriers across 347 origin airports, of which 5,655 were cancelled (0.924%) and 1,734 diverted (0.283%) [verified].

  DOT measures a flight's departure punctuality at the gate: "departure delay" is the actual gate-pushback time minus the scheduled gate time. What happens next — the taxi from gate to runway and the wait in the takeoff queue — is recorded separately, as "taxi-out" time, and does not enter the on-time departure figure. In May, the national mean taxi-out was 18.46 minutes and the median 16.0 [verified].

  Of the 368,670 flights that pushed back from the gate on time or early (departure delay ≤ 0), 33,147 — 8.99% — went on to record a taxi-out of 30 minutes or more before wheels-up [verified]. Applying DOT's own "not delayed" cutoff instead (off the gate fewer than 15 minutes late), 471,659 flights qualify and 9.18% taxied 30 minutes or more [verified].

  The concentration is by airport. Among origins with 3,000-plus operated departures, the share of on-time-or-early gate departures that then taxied 30 minutes or more was: Chicago O'Hare (ORD) 33.3%, with a median taxi-out of 25 minutes for those on-time departures; New York JFK 31.0%; New York LaGuardia (LGA) 23.5%; San Francisco (SFO) 23.4% [verified]. At the other end: St. Louis (STL) 1.46%, median taxi-out 11 minutes; Houston Hobby (HOU) 1.19%; Chicago Midway (MDW) 2.03% [verified]. Atlanta (ATL), the third-busiest origin in the file, ran 4.49%, median 15 [verified].
analysis: |
  The following is analysis, not fact. The number a traveler is sold — "on-time departure" — is a gate event. It certifies that the aircraft door closed and the plane left the stand on schedule. It says nothing about whether the plane then joined a 20-deep conga line for the runway. In May 2026, at O'Hare, a flight that pushed back on time or early was, one time in three, still on the ground 30 or more minutes later [verified]. Its passengers experienced a punctual departure and a half-hour of tarmac.

  The gap between airports is not a gap in volume. O'Hare was the single busiest origin in the file, with 33,393 operated departures; Atlanta and Denver ran close behind, at 27,210 and 27,328 — yet an on-time O'Hare pushback was more than seven times as likely to eat 30-plus tarmac minutes as an on-time Atlanta one (33.3% vs 4.49%), and 23 times as likely as an on-time St. Louis one (1.46%) [verified]. The median on-time O'Hare departure spent 25 minutes taxiing; the median on-time St. Louis departure, 11 — a 14-minute difference between two flights that both, on the government's books, "left on time" [verified]. What separates them is runway geometry and traffic density at the surface, not the punctuality of the crews.

  May matters to the reading: it is a low-cancellation, low-de-icing month (0.924% cancelled, against the 1.55% of the prior December this desk examined), so the tarmac time here is overwhelmingly congestion and airport layout, not snow being sprayed off wings [verified]. And the padding works: only 3.69% of on-time-gate departures went on to arrive 15 or more minutes late [verified]. Airlines budget the taxi into scheduled block time, so a long crawl to the runway rarely shows up as a late arrival. That is precisely why it is invisible — the delay is real minutes lived on the aircraft, absorbed by the schedule before it can dent any published statistic.
disagreement: |
  The strongest counter is that taxi-out is not "delay" and calling it one is a category error. DOT does not treat taxi time as a departure delay because, at a congested hub, 20-plus minutes of taxi is the normal, expected cost of operating there, already written into the timetable. A skeptic can argue the honest denominator is arrivals: if 96.3% of on-time-gate departures still land within 15 minutes of schedule, the tarmac time is a cost the airline pre-paid on the traveler's behalf, not a failure [verified].

  Three more cautions. The 30-minute threshold is this desk's cut; a 20- or 45-minute line would move every share. Taxi-out conflates distance with congestion — O'Hare's and JFK's runways sit far from their gates, so part of the 25-minute median is geography, not a queue, while compact St. Louis is short on both counts. And one month is one month: May 2026 is a specific traffic and weather draw, not a verdict on any airport's year. The defensible claim is narrow — that the on-time departure statistic, by construction, stops measuring at the gate, and that in May 2026 the unmeasured minutes after the gate were concentrated at a handful of airports.
viewFrom: |
  This comparison is possible only because U.S. law publishes flight-level taxi-out times that anyone can re-pull. Elsewhere the same operational reality is tracked but not exposed the same way: EUROCONTROL reports additional taxi-out time as a monthly airport-level average — a modeled "excess" over an unimpeded reference taxi — rather than a per-flight record, and most national authorities publish nothing comparable at all. The metric a European traveler can see is the airport's average congestion; the metric a U.S. traveler can see is their own flight's number, once someone reads all 611,735 of them at once.

humanWouldMiss: |
  A traveler whose flight leaves the gate at the scheduled minute counts it a punctual departure, and the airline counts it the same way — because the clock both are watching stops at pushback. Reading all 611,735 May flights at once shows what that clock cannot: nearly 9% of the country's on-time-or-early departures were still on the ground 30 minutes later, and at O'Hare it was one in three. The tarmac time never becomes a late arrival, because the schedule already swallowed it — which is exactly why it goes uncounted. The most honest number about a departure is not whether the door closed on time; it is how long after that the wheels left the ground, and that number only exists once someone reads the runway, not just the gate.
