facts: |
  California's Ralph M. Brown Act requires a legislative body to post the agenda for a regular meeting at least 72 hours before it begins (Cal. Gov. Code § 54954.2), and at least 24 hours before a special meeting (§ 54956). The Granicus Legistar InSite API, which many California cities use to run their public-meeting calendars, exposes for each meeting a field, `EventAgendaLastPublishedUTC`, recording when the agenda was last published. It requires no key.

  Pulled this run (retrieved 2026-08-09) for the 12 months from 2025-08-01 to 2026-08-09, filtered to meetings that carried a published-agenda timestamp and were not cancelled, five California cities' calendars record the following. Oakland, Mountain View, Hayward, Culver City, and Cupertino together listed 81 distinct public bodies holding 615 regular meetings and 33 special meetings over the window [verified]. (We excluded 12 meetings whose agenda timestamp fell *after* the meeting date — post-meeting re-publications — and report the rest.)

  Of the 615 regular meetings, 79 (12.8%) had their final agenda published less than 72 hours before the meeting's scheduled start [verified]. Of the 33 special meetings, 31 were published at least 24 hours out; 2 were inside 24 hours [verified]. The city-by-city share of regular meetings published inside 72 hours ranged from 6.9% in Mountain View (11 of 160) to 29.4% in Oakland (32 of 109); Hayward was 11.9% (21 of 176), Cupertino 11.8% (2 of 17), and Culver City 8.5% (13 of 153) [verified]. Median lead times were long everywhere — 4.2 days in Oakland, and 5.0 to 6.4 days in the other four [verified].

  Oakland's 32 inside-72-hour regular meetings were all the same body: the Rules & Legislation Committee, the standing committee that sets what reaches the full Council's agenda [verified]. Counting its one additional special meeting, the committee met 33 times in the window, and every meeting's final agenda was published between 18.0 and 21.6 hours before it began, a median of 20.3 hours [verified]. Oakland's other 16 bodies held 77 regular meetings over the same year; all 77 published a final agenda at least 72 hours out [verified].
analysis: |
  The following is analysis, not fact. The Brown Act's 72-hour rule is the floor for how much notice a resident gets to read what their government is about to decide and decide whether to show up. Across these five cities the floor is mostly cleared with room to spare — the typical regular agenda lands 4 to 6 days ahead. The finding is not a systemic notice failure. It is where the short-notice meetings concentrate, and that concentration is not random.

  Nearly half of every inside-72-hour regular meeting in the five-city set — 32 of 79 — is one committee in one city [verified]. And it is a specific committee: the body that decides which items advance to the Oakland City Council's agenda. The one meeting a resident would most want early sight of, because it is where the Council's own docket is shaped, is the one whose final agenda posts about a day before it meets, week after week.

  The consistency is the tell. Across 33 meetings the lead time never left an 18.0-to-21.6-hour band [verified]. That tightness is more consistent with a routine, single day-before posting than with an original agenda posted earlier and amended late — sporadic amendments would scatter, not cluster within three and a half hours every week [modeled]. Read against the city's own behavior, the pattern is sharper still: every one of Oakland's other 16 bodies cleared 72 hours on every one of 77 meetings [verified]. Oakland can and does post early — for the agenda-setting committee, it does not.

  The cross-city numbers also show what a single city's record cannot: that a 12.8% inside-72-hour rate is not a fact about "California cities" but a blend of very different postures. Mountain View posts late 6.9% of the time and its late meetings are scattered across advisory bodies; Oakland's 29.4% is one committee. The same statutory floor produces two unrelated shapes, and only reading every body across every city at once separates them.
disagreement: |
  The strongest counter is that `EventAgendaLastPublishedUTC` records the *last* time an agenda was published, not the first. A lead time under 72 hours can mean the original agenda was posted on time and a later version — a corrected typo, an added attachment, a revised staff report — was republished inside the window. On this reading a short "last published" time proves only that the *final* version residents could rely on was not settled 72 hours out, not that notice was legally deficient. That caveat is real and we cannot rule it out from the API alone; only each city's original posting records can. It is why we frame every figure here as the publication timestamp against the 72-hour reference, and assert no violation of the statute by any body.

  Two further cautions. First, the Brown Act's 72-hour rule governs *regular* meetings; special meetings need only 24 hours. Whether a given standing committee's sitting is legally "regular" or "special" is a classification we cannot always read from the API — though Oakland's own records distinguish them, labeling one Rules & Legislation sitting a "Special" meeting and the other 32 regular, which is why we count those 32 against the 72-hour line. Second, the timestamps are UTC and meeting times are local; we converted with a daylight-saving-aware Pacific offset, leaving roughly an hour of uncertainty near the boundary. No claim here rests on that hour — the Rules Committee cluster sits ~50 hours inside the line, and Hayward's two near-zero City Council cases (final agendas published 0.1 and 0.3 hours before the meeting) sit far on the other side of any rounding.
viewFrom: |
  From outside California the specific number, 72 hours, is parochial; the method is not. Every state has an open-meetings statute with a posting deadline, and any government that runs its calendar on a platform exposing a publication timestamp can be measured against its own state's floor the same way. What is legible in Oakland is legible anywhere the timestamp is public: not whether an agenda exists, but how long before the vote it was allowed to. The un-copyable move is not reading Oakland — it is reading the publication clock on every body in every city on the platform at once, so that a single committee's standing habit surfaces against 80 other bodies that do it differently.
