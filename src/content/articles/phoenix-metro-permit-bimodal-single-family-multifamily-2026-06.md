facts: |
  The Census Bureau's Building Permits Survey publishes, free and monthly, the count of new housing units authorized by every permit-issuing place in the country, broken out by building size: 1-unit, 2-unit, 3-4 unit, and 5-or-more-unit structures. We pulled the West Region place file for year-to-date January–June 2026 (retrieved 2026-08-08) and isolated the 32 jurisdictions the survey assigns to the Phoenix-Mesa-Chandler, AZ metro (CBSA 38060).

  Those 32 jurisdictions permitted 17,059 housing units in the first six months of 2026 [verified]. Of that total, 11,989 units were in single-family (1-unit) structures — 70% — and 4,574 were in buildings of 5 units or more — 27% [verified]. Structures of 2 to 4 units, the "missing middle," accounted for 496 units, or 2.9% of everything permitted [verified].

  The apartment total is concentrated in one place. The city of Phoenix permitted 2,696 units in 5+ structures — 59% of every such unit in the entire metro — against 1,516 single-family units of its own [verified]. Add Mesa (497 units in 5+ structures) and Gilbert (283) and three jurisdictions hold 76% of the metro's apartment permitting [verified].

  At the other pole, 9 jurisdictions each permitted 100 or more housing units while authorizing zero units in any 5+ structure: Avondale (422 units), Buckeye (1,372), Coolidge (118), Florence (241), Glendale (271), Goodyear (775), Maricopa County unincorporated (794), the city of Maricopa (552), and Queen Creek (636) [verified]. Buckeye, at 1,372 units, was the metro's third-largest permitter of housing overall — and every one of those permits was for a detached house or a duplex, none for an apartment building [verified].

  Metro-wide, permitting is down. The same 32-jurisdiction footprint permitted 20,364 units in the first half of 2025; the 17,059 figure for the first half of 2026 is a 16% decline year-over-year [verified].
analysis: |
  The following is analysis, not fact. The single most quoted number here — "27% multifamily" — is the least descriptive. It is a metro average that corresponds to almost no jurisdiction inside the metro. Housing here is not built by cities that each strike some balance between houses and apartments; it is built by cities that do one thing or the other.

  The clearest way to see it is to set aside the imputation. Of the 32 jurisdictions, 23 reported all six months of 2026 to the survey (the other 9 carry Census estimates for the months they did not report — see Room for Disagreement) [verified]. Among those 23 full reporters, 15 permitted 50 or more units. Sort those 15 by their apartment share: 11 of them fall at or below 10% multifamily (8,432 units, overwhelmingly detached houses), 3 fall at or above 50% multifamily (Phoenix, Mesa, and Gilbert; 5,703 units), and exactly 1 — Peoria, at 44% — lands anywhere in the middle (756 units) [verified]. Ninety-five percent of the housing permitted by these sizable, fully reporting jurisdictions came from a city sitting at one extreme of the distribution [modeled]. The middle is empty at the level of the number and at the level of the city producing it.

  This is what a permit map, rather than a permit total, reveals. The metro's growth is territorial: the built-out core and the two inner suburbs directly east of it (Mesa, Gilbert) absorb the density, while the fringe cities on the western and southeastern edges — Buckeye, Goodyear, Surprise, Queen Creek, Maricopa — grow almost exclusively outward in detached houses. A resident who reads "Phoenix metro is 27% multifamily" and pictures a metro gradually densifying everywhere has the wrong picture. The metro is densifying in three jurisdictions and sprawling in the rest, and the average of those two behaviors is a city that does not exist.
disagreement: |
  The strongest objection is that these are permits, not completions, and not occupancy. A permit authorizes construction; it does not guarantee the unit gets built, and multifamily projects in particular can be permitted in a lumpy, all-at-once fashion that a six-month window catches or misses by accident. A single 300-unit apartment approval landing in July instead of June would move a city across our threshold. The figures describe intent to build as recorded by permit, not housing delivered [verified]; a longer window smooths this, and we report a fixed half-year.

  The second, larger caution is imputation. The Building Permits Survey estimates activity for places that do not report in a given month, and 9 of the 32 jurisdictions here reported fewer than six months in 2026 [verified]. The most consequential case is Tempe, which reported zero months this year — its entire 501-unit, 91%-multifamily figure is a Census estimate, not a filing [verified]. For that reason Tempe is excluded from the full-reporter analysis above, and the three-jurisdiction multifamily core (Phoenix, Mesa, Gilbert) rests only on places that reported every month. The bimodal pattern holds with the imputed places removed; it does not depend on them.

  Third, "zero apartments permitted" is not the same as "apartments prohibited." A city can permit no 5+ structures in a half-year because none were proposed, because a large project cleared the prior year, or because of zoning — the data cannot distinguish these, and we assert none. We report the count and the distribution; we do not assign a cause or a motive to any city's number.
viewFrom: |
  From the vantage of the individual homebuyer, the distribution is the story the average hides. If you are looking for an apartment or a townhouse, the metro's 4,574 multifamily permits are functionally three addresses: Phoenix, Mesa, Gilbert. If you are looking for a new detached house, the supply is on the edges — Buckeye, Goodyear, Queen Creek, Maricopa — where 100% of new permits are for exactly that. The metro-wide ratio is a number no household actually shops in.

  From the vantage of the roughly 90,000 local governments the survey covers, Phoenix is an easy case: a large metro whose places nearly all report, letting the whole permit map be assembled at once. Most metros are patchier, and most of this record — every place, every month, every structure size — is never read across jurisdictions at all, because no single desk covers 32 city halls.

humanWouldMiss: |
  A beat reporter covers one groundbreaking: a 300-unit tower in downtown Phoenix, a master-planned subdivision in Buckeye. Each is true and each is unrepresentative, because the story is not any single project but the shape of all 17,059 permits at once. Reading the whole half-year across all 32 jurisdictions, the thing no single-city story can show is that the metro has no typical city. Its 27% multifamily average is a statistical center with almost nothing near it: 11 sizable jurisdictions build essentially only houses, 3 build mostly apartments, and just one — Peoria — lands in between. The number a human would quote is the one number that describes none of the places it summarizes. And the concentration cuts the other way too: strip out the city of Phoenix and the entire rest of the metro — 31 jurisdictions holding roughly 3.3 million residents by the survey's own population column — permitted just 1,878 apartment units in six months, fewer than Phoenix permitted by itself.
