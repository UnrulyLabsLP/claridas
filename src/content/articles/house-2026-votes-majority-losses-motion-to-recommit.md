facts: |
  The Office of the Clerk of the U.S. House of Representatives publishes every recorded floor vote as a structured XML file, one per roll call, at a stable URL (`clerk.house.gov/evs/2026/rollNNN.xml`). Each file carries the roll-call number, date, measure number, the question voted on, the result, and vote totals broken out by party. We pulled all 283 roll-call files for the second session of the 119th Congress this run — roll 1 (January 6, 2026) through roll 283 (July 23, 2026) — retrieved 2026-08-10, and reduced each to its metadata and party totals [verified]. Roll 1 was a quorum call; the other 282 are substantive recorded votes and are the basis for every figure below. The XML metadata labels the Republican party as the chamber majority (`<majority>R</majority>`) [verified].

  The 282 votes were not evenly contested. The median winning margin was 17 votes and the mean was 114.6 — a spread that reflects a bimodal record: 91 votes (32.3%) were decided by 100 votes or more, while 87 (30.9%) were decided by 5 or fewer, 115 (40.8%) by 10 or fewer, 14 by a single vote, and three more ended in exact ties that therefore failed — H CON RES 68 at 215–215 (roll 48), H CON RES 75 at 212–212 (roll 170), and a motion to recommit on H.R. 1329 at 209–209 (roll 187); those 14 one-vote margins plus the 3 ties are the 17 votes decided by a single vote or fewer [verified].

  Sorting each vote by where the majority of each party's voting members stood: the two parties' majorities were on opposite sides on 202 of 282 votes (71.6%), and on the same side on 80 (28.4%) [verified]. Fifty-nine votes were motions to suspend the rules — the House's two-thirds-threshold track for less-contested measures — and on 56 of those 59 both parties' majorities agreed [verified].

  On the 202 party-opposed votes, the nominal-majority (Republican) side was on the winning side 160 times and the losing side 42 (20.8%) [verified]. Those 42 losses break down as: 20 amendment votes, 9 special rules or resolutions, 7 votes on final passage, 4 discharge motions, and 2 suspension motions [verified]. Two other party-opposed votes were attempts to override a presidential veto that fell short of the two-thirds required — H.R. 504 at 236–188 (roll 8) and H.R. 131 at 248–177 (roll 9); on each the Republican majority voted nay to sustain the veto, so its side prevailed and both count as majority wins, not losses [verified]. Three of the losses were special rules that failed on the floor — the resolutions a majority ordinarily needs to bring its own bills up for debate: H RES 1042 (214–217, roll 60), H RES 1175 (197–228, roll 124), and H RES 1398 (198–224, roll 231) [verified]. Four were discharge motions that passed over the majority leadership's opposition, each carried by a unanimous minority plus a single-digit number of majority-party members: rolls 4, 113, 200, and 212 [verified].

  Separately, the motion to recommit — the minority's one procedurally guaranteed motion before final passage — was offered 36 times and failed all 36, the closest being the 209–209 tie on roll 187 [verified]. The motion to order the previous question, the majority's procedural tool for closing debate on a rule, was voted 26 times, passed all 26, and was party-opposed on every one, at a median margin of 2 votes [verified].
analysis: |
  The following is analysis, not fact. A day's coverage of the House reports the vote in front of it: a rule adopted, a bill passed, a discharge petition ripening. Read as a set, the 282 recorded votes of the 2026 session describe a chamber operating in two separate registers, and the interesting one is not the one that makes news.

  The first register is a bipartisan lane that barely registers as conflict: 59 suspension votes, 56 of them with both parties' majorities on the same side, moving measures that clear a two-thirds bar by design. The second is the contested lane — 202 votes, 71.6% of the total, where the parties' majorities opposed each other. Inside that lane the arithmetic is unusually tight. Nearly a third of all recorded votes were decided by five or fewer, three landed on exact ties, and the median party-line procedural vote — ordering the previous question — carried by two. A margin distribution this bimodal (a third of votes decided by 100-plus, a third by 5 or fewer) is the signature of a chamber that either agrees overwhelmingly or splits to the last member, with little in between.

  In that tight lane the majority's control was real but not automatic: it lost 42 party-opposed votes, and the losses cluster in the two places control is supposed to be structural. Three special rules — the vehicles a majority uses to schedule its own floor — failed outright, and four discharge motions succeeded, each requiring a handful of majority-party members to join a united minority to reach the threshold. The mechanism of those losses is what a full read exposes: they did not come through the minority's formal counter-tool. The motion to recommit, the one motion the minority is guaranteed before passage, went 0-for-36. None of the 42 losses came through it; they came as failed rules, successful discharge motions, and amendment and passage votes decided in a chamber split so finely that absences and a handful of crossovers were often the difference. The pattern is consistent with leverage that is arithmetic rather than procedural — a near-tied floor, not the rulebook. The record shows the counts; it does not identify why any member voted as they did, and this piece asserts none.
disagreement: |
  The strongest cautions are about classification and about what "the majority lost" can mean. "Loss" here is defined narrowly and mechanically: the majority of the majority party's *voting* members ended up on the losing side. That measure counts an amendment the majority opposed but that passed as a "loss," and 20 of the 42 are amendment votes — some of which carry no leadership position at all, so reading all 42 as defeats of the majority's agenda overstates the case. The robust core is the smaller, unambiguous set: the 3 special rules that failed and the 4 discharge motions that carried, where the majority's institutional control was directly at stake.

  Second, margins are shaped by attendance as much as by preference. Roll 60's rule failed 214–217 with 214 Republicans voting yes; the loss turned partly on absences, not only on crossovers. A near-tied result is not evidence of a durable coalition — it can reflect who was in the chamber that hour.

  Third, this is a partial session (January 6 to July 23) and party membership shifted over the window through the ordinary turnover of resignations and special elections, so any single seat count is a snapshot; we report per-vote party totals rather than a fixed roster. The "majority" label is the Clerk's own metadata field, not our characterization. And the categories — what counts as a "special rule," a "discharge motion," a "party-opposed" vote — are our tabulation of the Clerk's question text; a reader re-pulling the files can reproduce or contest each bucket.

Each is accurate on its own, and none of them, alone, is unusual. What only the whole set shows is the pattern the individual reports cannot: that the minority's formal, guaranteed procedural motion never once succeeded across 36 tries, and that the majority nonetheless lost 42 contests — meaning the friction in the 2026 House ran along the arithmetic of a near-tied floor rather than through the rulebook either side is written to use. The U.S. House has no mechanism that registers a chamber this closely divided — losing this many of its own scheduling votes — as a single event, so the same condition surfaces only as a scatter of one-day stories — and as a vote ledger that has to be read in full to be seen.
