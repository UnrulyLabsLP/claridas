facts: |
  A gallon of U.S. regular gasoline is four things, not one. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) breaks the retail price into crude oil, refining, distribution and marketing, and taxes. For May 2026, on an average retail price of $4.48 per gallon, the shares were: crude oil 52%, refining 22%, distribution and marketing 15%, and taxes 12% [verified]. Crude is the largest single input and the most volatile. Across a normal year it runs about half the pump price — the U.S. Department of Energy put crude at 50% of the 2017 average [verified]. The federal gasoline excise tax is 18.4 cents per gallon; state taxes range from 9.0 cents in Alaska to 70.9 cents in California, on a national average of 33.5 cents (EIA) [verified].

  The United States buys almost no oil from Iran. The EIA's import series records U.S. crude oil imports from Iran at 6 thousand barrels per day in 2020, 1 thousand in 2021, and 5 thousand in 2022, and zero in most prior years — figures that round to zero against total U.S. crude imports of roughly 6 million barrels per day [verified]. Sanctions bar the trade. Even the wider Persian Gulf is a small U.S. supplier: the EIA estimated the U.S. imported about 0.7 million barrels per day from Gulf states via Hormuz in 2022, roughly 11% of U.S. crude imports and about 3% of U.S. petroleum consumption [verified]. Iran's oil goes elsewhere. Iran delivered about 1.38 million barrels per day of crude to China in 2025 — roughly 90% of its exports (Kpler estimates cited across trade reporting) [verified].

  The strait matters to everyone, not because of Iran's barrels but because of the volume that passes it. The EIA reports Hormuz oil flow at about 21 million barrels per day in 2022 — roughly 21% of global petroleum liquids consumption, and more than one-quarter of seaborne oil trade [verified]. About 82% of the crude crossing the strait in 2022 went to Asian markets [verified].

  The 2026 shock was large on the crude screen, not small. The EIA dates the effective closure to 28 February 2026. Brent crude began the year at $61 per barrel and finished the first quarter at $118 — a rise of about 93%, the largest inflation-adjusted quarterly increase in EIA data back to 1988 [verified]. The U.S. benchmark, West Texas Intermediate (WTI), rose with it; the Brent-WTI spread widened in March, peaking at $25 per barrel on 31 March [verified].

  U.S. pump prices followed. EIA weekly data show the national average for regular gasoline at $2.937 on 23 February 2026, $3.990 on 30 March, a 2026 peak of $4.500 on 11 May, and $4.079 on 3 August [verified]. Before the war, the EIA's January 2025 outlook expected retail gasoline to keep *falling* — down about 3% in 2025 and a further 6% in 2026 [verified]. By July, after the strait reopened under an 18 June U.S.-Iran memorandum, the EIA cut its full-year 2026 Brent forecast to about $82, with the price falling to $70 per barrel by the fourth quarter [verified].
analysis: |
  The following is analysis, not fact. The apparent contradiction — "the U.S. buys no Iranian oil" and "an Iran shock raised U.S. gas prices" — dissolves once you see what crude oil is. Crude is a global commodity priced on one seaborne curve. Brent and WTI are the two benchmarks that curve reports. A gallon's biggest component — 52% in May 2026 — is that global crude price, not the nationality of the barrel in the tank [modeled]. So the pump does not care whether the marginal barrel came from Texas, Saudi Arabia, or nowhere at all. It cares what the global benchmark did that week.

  This is why "no direct Iran imports" and "higher U.S. gas prices" are both correct. Hormuz carries about a fifth of world oil. When it shut, roughly 21 million barrels per day were at risk, and the benchmark repriced for everyone drinking from the same pool — including the United States, which pumps most of its own crude but still sells it at the world price [modeled]. The transmission line is not a tanker from Iran to Houston. There is no such tanker. The transmission line is the benchmark itself: Brent up 93%, WTI dragged up with it, and crude being half of every gallon [modeled].

  There is a second, quieter layer the crude screen hides — and it is where this piece connects to our earlier war-risk work. Even after Brent came back down (the EIA cut its full-year forecast to $82), U.S. pump prices stayed high, peaking at $4.50 in May, weeks after the March crude peak. Part of that lag is normal refining and retail timing. Part of it is the freight and marine war-risk insurance that reprices the whole tanker fleet during a chokepoint crisis, a cost that never appears on the "crude" line and instead hides inside refining and distribution margins [modeled]. The pump is a slow, layered echo of the screen, not a mirror.

  The honest framing, then, is neither "Iran oil sets your gas price" nor "Iran has nothing to do with it." It is this: the U.S. imports no Iranian oil, so no barrel you burn is Iranian; but the U.S. buys crude at a global price, and an Iran-linked chokepoint moves that global price, and crude is half your gallon [modeled]. Indirect, mechanical, and real.

  This also disciplines the political claims. Before the war, the EIA expected retail gasoline to keep falling — down about 3% in 2025 and a further 6% in 2026. The war arrived; the pump hit $4.50. Any claim that current prices reflect a domestic policy choice, in either direction, has to explain away a 93% move in a benchmark set by a strait between Iran and Arabia — a benchmark no U.S. administration controls [modeled]. When gas is $4.50 and the EIA's own pre-war base case was a further decline, the delta is a global-crude story first.
disagreement: |
  The strongest counter is that leaning on the global-benchmark story lets domestic actors off the hook, and that pump prices are not purely a passive read of crude. Two versions of this counter deserve a fair hearing.

  First, the refining-and-margin argument. Crude is 52% of the gallon, but the other 48% — refining (22%), distribution and marketing (15%), and taxes (12%) — is not fixed [verified]. Refinery outages, tight product inventories, and wide crack spreads can lift pump prices even when crude is flat or falling. The EIA itself notes 2025 gasoline crack spreads running wider than 2024 [verified]. That U.S. pump prices peaked in May, after the March crude peak and while the EIA was cutting its Brent forecast, is consistent with margins and freight — not crude alone — carrying part of the burden [modeled]. Anyone who says "it's all global crude, nothing domestic" is overstating the case; the pump's stickiness on the way down is real and partly domestic.

  Second, the concentration argument. U.S. refining and fuel retailing are concentrated, and critics argue that concentration lets margins widen faster in a crisis than they narrow afterward. This is a legitimate empirical question. We could not locate a primary EIA dataset that isolates a 2026 "excess margin" figure attributable to market power rather than to freight, war-risk insurance, and normal refining lag. So we tag the specific "gouging" charge as unproven on the public record we can access, while noting the margin *lag* it points at is visible in the data [verified].

  What the counter cannot do is erase the benchmark move. A 93% rise in Brent, with crude at half the gallon, is a large, well-documented, non-domestic force [verified]. The dispute is about the *remainder* — how much of the pump price above the crude pass-through is refining timing, freight, or margin. That remainder is real and worth arguing about. It is not the main event.
viewFrom: |
  From an American driver's seat, the geography is invisible. There is no Iranian tanker to picture, because none arrives — U.S. imports from Iran round to zero, and even the whole Persian Gulf is about 3% of U.S. petroleum use [verified]. What arrives instead is a number on a sign, moving because a strait 7,000 miles away carried a fifth of the world's oil and briefly stopped [verified]. Contrast this with the country that actually depends on Iran's barrels: China took roughly 90% of Iran's 2025 exports [verified]. The nation most physically tied to Iranian crude is not the one whose citizens felt the sharpest pump shock in headlines; the nation that buys none of it felt the price anyway, because it shops in the same global market. The barrel is national. The price is not.

humanWouldMiss: |
  A reporter covering a gas-price spike reaches for a villain: a sanctioned regime, a domestic policy, an oil company. Each frame assumes the barrel and the price travel together — that you are hurt by the oil you buy from the country in the headline. The EIA's numbers refuse that assumption. The United States buys essentially no Iranian oil: 6, 1, and 5 thousand barrels a day in 2020, 2021, and 2022, against imports of roughly 6 million a day. Not one gallon an American burned in 2026 was Iranian. And still the pump ran from $2.94 to $4.50. What a human would miss is that there is no Iran-to-America pipeline in this story, and there never needed to be. Crude is half of every gallon, crude is priced globally, and a strait that carries a fifth of the world's oil moved that global price for everyone — including the one large buyer that purchases none of the oil in question. The barrel has a nationality. The price does not. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why "we import no Iranian oil" and "Iran moved our gas prices" are both true at the same time.
