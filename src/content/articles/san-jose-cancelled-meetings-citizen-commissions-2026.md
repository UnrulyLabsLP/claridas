facts: |
  The Granicus Legistar InSite API exposes the City of San José's full meeting calendar — every noticed meeting of every body, with the body's name, the meeting date, and a comment field the clerk uses to mark cancellations — with no key or login. Pulled this run (retrieved 2026-08-09) for a fixed 12-month window, August 1, 2025 through July 31, 2026, the record is as follows.

  The API returns 554 meeting entries in the window [verified]. Two of those entries are not deliberative bodies but the platform's catch-all agenda containers — "Miscellaneous Agendas" (113 entries) and "NOTICES" (1) — and are set aside here [verified]. That leaves 440 noticed meetings of the city's actual councils, boards, commissions, and standing committees [verified]. Of those 440, the clerk marked 73 CANCELLED in the meeting comment — 16.6%, roughly one in six — leaving 367 held [verified].

  The cancellations are not spread evenly. The elected City Council noticed 58 regular meetings and cancelled 3 (5.2%) [verified]. The agenda-setting Rules and Open Government Committee noticed 42 and cancelled none [verified]. Every body other than the City Council, taken together, cancelled 70 of 382 noticed meetings (18.3%) [verified], and the highest rates sit on the appointed boards and volunteer citizen commissions: the Board of Fair Campaign and Political Practices cancelled 10 of 14 (71.4%); the Historic Landmarks Commission 15 of 23 (65.2%, of which 12 were its Design Review Committee sittings); the Downtown Parking Board 2 of 4 (50.0%, a small sample); the Senior Citizens Commission 6 of 15 (40.0%); the Housing and Community Development Commission 4 of 11 (36.4%); the Civil Service Commission 6 of 26 (23.1%); the Planning Commission 8 of 39 (20.5%) [verified].

  Separately, the API's /bodies endpoint lists 42 bodies, 38 of them flagged active; 29 of those active bodies held at least one noticed meeting in the window — the same 29 deliberative bodies ranked here, once the two non-body agenda containers (Miscellaneous Agendas and NOTICES) are set aside [verified].
analysis: |
  The following is analysis, not fact. A cancelled meeting is a routine, lawful event: a body with no time-sensitive business, or one that cannot reach quorum on a given night, cancels rather than convenes, and California's open-meeting law is satisfied so long as the cancellation is noticed. Nothing in these counts assigns a cause to any cancellation, and none is implied. The finding is one of distribution — the cancellations are concentrated, and where they concentrate is legible only when the whole year's calendar is read at once.

  The concentration is the story. A resident who tracks San José's government by watching the City Council sees a body that meets as scheduled: 55 of 58 nights held, a 5.2% cancellation rate [verified]. But the City Council is one body among dozens, and its 5.2% rate ranks 16th of the 29 bodies that met — 15 bodies cancelled nothing at all, among them the agenda-setting Rules and Open Government Committee across all 42 of its meetings. The bodies designed to bring residents formally into the process — the appointed commissions where volunteers hear historic-preservation cases, campaign-finance complaints, senior-services questions, housing policy — are the ones whose meetings are called off, at 36% to 71% [verified]. Two commissions convened fewer than half the times they told the public they would.

  The Historic Landmarks Commission is the cleanest illustration [verified]. It noticed 23 sessions and cancelled 15; 12 of those 15 were its twice-monthly Design Review Committee — the sitting that reviews alterations to designated historic properties. A body that publishes a calendar of two meetings a month met, in practice, closer to once. The full commission mostly held; the working sub-body mostly did not. From a single meeting notice this is invisible; across a year of them it is the dominant pattern of the body.

  What the record does not carry is why. The comment field says CANCELLED; it does not say whether the cause was an empty agenda, a failed quorum, a staffing gap, or a scheduling convention. That absence is the boundary of the finding: the pattern is precise, the explanation is not in the feed.
disagreement: |
  The strongest counter is that a cancellation rate measures supply of meetings, not health of a body, and the two can move opposite ways. A commission that cancels when it has nothing before it is being responsible with volunteers' evenings, not neglecting its charge; a body that convened every noticed night regardless of agenda would be padding, not governing. On this reading a 71% cancellation rate at the Board of Fair Campaign and Political Practices is consistent with a city that simply had few campaign-finance matters in an off-cycle year — and the record here cannot refute that, because it carries the cancellation but not the docket that would or would not have filled the meeting.

  A second caution is on the counts themselves. "Cancelled" is the clerk's own keystroke in a free-text comment; a meeting called off but not marked, or marked in words we did not match, would be miscounted, and a meeting cancelled and then re-noticed appears twice, inflating the denominator. We match the string CANCELLED and report noticed-meeting cancellation rates, not a census of intent. Likewise the nine flagged-active bodies that show no meeting in the window are reported as a data-hygiene observation, not a roster of defunct boards: a flag left set on a dormant or renamed body — the "Ped Advisory" entry duplicates a committee that met nine times under its full name — produces the same empty result. The robust finding is the uneven cancellation rate across bodies that did meet; the rest is caveated.
