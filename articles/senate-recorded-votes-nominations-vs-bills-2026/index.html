No desk reads every roll call end to end; we did. Through vote 231 on August 8, nomination business drew 94 of the 231 — 40.7%. The Facts

A gallery reporter sees one vote at a time and covers the ones that make news — a close confirmation, a contested bill. We read all 231 recorded votes of the session at once, and the shape only the complete read shows is this: nomination business drew 94 of them; the final passage of a bill drew 6. The U.S. Senate publishes every recorded vote of the 119th Congress, 2nd session, in a single machine-readable file. As of this run (retrieved 2026-08-11, re-verified 2026-08-15) it lists 231 votes, numbered 1 through 231 with no gaps and no duplicates, spanning January 5 to August 8, 2026. Ninety-four of the 231 — 40.7% — were roll calls on nomination business. Those 94 break down as 49 final confirmation votes, 44 cloture motions on nominations, and 1 motion to proceed. Every confirmation vote resulted in confirmation; none was rejected. Now hold the two units apart, because this is where a single seat and the full ledger diverge. The 49 confirmation votes confirmed 170 nominations, not 49, because two of them were en-bloc votes. The count is of nomination matters — the Senate's PN records — not a headcount of distinct persons; the file lists PN matters, and one nominee can hold more than one. A single en-bloc roll call confirms many matters at once. Vote 125 (May 18) confirmed 49 PN matters under S. Res. 690; vote 225 (August 7) confirmed 74 PN matters under S. Res. 817. The other 47 confirmation votes each confirmed one matter. So 47 individual confirmations plus 123 en-bloc confirmations equal 170 distinct PN matters confirmed, none of the PN numbers overlapping. Of the 47 individually confirmed matters, 42 were preceded by a separate cloture vote to close debate; 5 cleared on a single recorded vote. Vote 230, on August 8, confirmed the Attorney General 50–49; vote 229 was the cloture motion that preceded it. Final passage of a bill accounts for 6 of the 231 roll calls — H.R. 6500, H.R. 5334, S. 2, H.R. 6644, H.R. 7148 and H.R. 6938, all passed. Widening the frame to every final legislative-type disposition reaches 12 votes: those 6 bill passages, 2 simple resolutions (votes 114 and 217), 2 concurrent resolutions (H.Con.Res. 86 and S.Con.Res. 33), and 2 joint resolutions (H.J.Res. 140 and H.J.Res. 142). The 2 simple resolutions are the executive resolutions — S. Res. 690 and S. Res. 817 — that authorized the two en-bloc nomination bundles. Set those 2 aside as nomination logistics and 10 of the 12 dispose of legislation, of which 6 pass a bill. Cloture — the vote to end debate, not to decide the underlying question — accounts for 70 of the 231 roll calls, or 30.3%: 56 agreed to, 14 rejected. Forty-four of those 70 cloture votes were on nominations.

The Analysis

The following is analysis, not fact. Read end to end, the 2026 recorded-vote record is a confirmation record with legislation at the margin. The single largest category of roll call we found is not passing a law or amending one. It is seating an executive or judicial officer, and often the cloture vote that has to happen first. The gap is wide on either unit you pick, and reading the whole set is what lets us say that rather than guess it. Counted as roll calls, nomination business drew 94 votes and bill passage drew 6 — about 16 to 1. Counted as outcomes, the Senate confirmed 170 nomination matters and passed 6 bills. The two counts point the same way, so the finding does not rest on one framing. mmodeled The en-bloc mechanism is the part one pass through the file hides, and the part that separates our two units. Two roll calls in 2026 cleared 123 nomination matters between them. That is why the number of matters confirmed (170) is far larger than the number of confirmation votes (49). A count that reads only the top-level record sees 47 confirmations and misses the two votes that did most of the work. The chamber built the machinery for this: votes 114 and 217 passed the two "executive resolutions" that let the Senate consider those nominations as a block. So even part of the legislative column — 2 of the 12 final dispositions — is nomination logistics. mmodeled The two-step still shows up in the individual confirmations. Of the 47 matters confirmed one at a time, 42 first drew a cloture vote to end debate — two roll calls to place one official. Read across the set, the friction the tally records is procedural rather than substantive: every confirmation vote resulted in confirmation, yet 42 of the 47 individual ones carried a debate-ending vote first. What the file shows is the tally, not the reason: each of those cloture motions cleared, and cleared by simple majority — vote 229, the cloture on the Attorney General, at 50–49; the two en-bloc cloture motions (votes 124 and 221) at 51–46 and 50–44. We report the sequence and the margins; we do not read a strategy into them. mmodeled The contrast with 6 bill-passage votes is real but must be read for what it is. It does not mean the Senate passed only 6 bills in 2026. It means 6 of its recorded votes were the final passage of a bill. Legislation and many uncontested nominations can move by voice vote or unanimous consent and never generate a roll call. So the recorded record is a sample — the residue of the questions that had to be counted — not the chamber's whole output. Within that sample, read whole, the roll calls in 2026 fall 94 to 6 toward nomination business over bill passage. mmodeled

Room for Disagreement

The strongest objection is a denominator objection, and it holds. Recorded votes are not the Senate's whole output; they are the residue of the questions that drew a roll call. A session that confirmed many nominees by unanimous consent and passed bills by voice would leave few roll calls behind, and none of that work appears in the 231. So "6 bills" is 6 recorded passage votes, not 6 laws — the count understates legislating and cannot be read as total productivity. We claim only what the recorded record shows: where the votes that had to be counted went. A second reading cuts against our own roll-call framing, and it is the sharper one. Count roll calls and nomination business runs 94 to 6 over bill passage — but the en-bloc votes cut the other way. Two roll calls confirmed 123 matters. That is the Senate moving nominations in bulk, with few votes. On the roll-call measure nomination business is the largest recorded category; on the matters measure it is work the chamber can clear fast. Reading the whole set forces us to hold both: many nomination roll calls, and a batching tool that keeps the vote count from being higher still. Finally, this is a partial-year, single-session window (January 5 to August 8, 2026) with the session still open; the mix can shift as an appropriations or year-end stretch adds legislative roll calls. We report the record through vote 231 and scope every figure to it. We assign no motive to either party's use of the Senate's rules; the cloture-then-confirm sequence and the en-bloc bundling are reported as procedural facts visible in the tally, not as anyone's strategy.

The View From

From the vantage of the vote ledger rather than the day's headline, a confirmation and a bill are the same unit — one roll call — and that flattening is what lets us read all 231 as one object and see the imbalance. A desk covers the Attorney General's 50–49 confirmation as an event; the ledger records it as vote 230, right after vote 229, the cloture motion that made it possible. But reading the ledger whole also surfaces what a single line hides: vote 225 was one line in the file, and it confirmed 74 nomination matters. The count of roll calls and the count of matters diverge exactly where the Senate decided to move nominations in a block.
