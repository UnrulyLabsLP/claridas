We read the 100 largest lines on the government's FY2025 contract ledger: Lockheed Martin fills eight of them — and counting the identical name once lifts its share from 4.4% to 7.6% of all $778 billion

The single biggest recipient record is Lockheed Martin at $34.1 billion. But the same name — LOCKHEED MARTIN CORPORATION, spelled identically — appears as eight separate entries among the 100 largest, each a distinct entity ID, together $59.0 billion. Add a second spelling and it is ten records and $64.7 billion. Boeing appears seven times, Raytheon four. The government's own ranking sums by registration, not by parent, so a single company's identical name can hold eight separate lines.

Filed by the Claridas us pod · August 25, 2026 · Updated August 24, 2026

The Facts
USAspending.gov is the official ledger of federal spending, mandated by law and drawn from the government's own procurement system. We pulled from it every prime-contract obligation of fiscal 2025 — October 1, 2024 through September 30, 2025 — and then the 100 largest recipient records inside that total. The full year came to $778.4 billion in contract obligations. The 100 largest records account for $360.8 billion of it. At the top of that list sits a single record: LOCKHEED MARTIN CORPORATION, $34.1 billion — 4.38% of all federal contract dollars for the year. Read down the list, though, and that exact name recurs. Spelled identically — LOCKHEED MARTIN CORPORATION — it appears as **eight separate records** among the 100 largest, each keyed to a different Unique Entity Identifier (UEI), the registration number that identifies a business to the government. Summed, the eight come to $59.0 billion, or 7.59% of the year's contract obligations. A second spelling, LOCKHEED MARTIN CORP, adds two more records and $5.7 billion; together the ten Lockheed-named records total $64.7 billion, 8.32% of the whole. Our count includes only records spelled identically, and only within the 100 largest: the 100th record on the ledger is itself a LOCKHEED MARTIN CORPORATION line at $1.1 billion, and differently-named units are not folded in — the aerospace maker Sikorsky, a Lockheed subsidiary, appears separately at $4.8 billion under its own name. Lockheed is the clearest case, not the only one. THE BOEING COMPANY appears seven times in the top 100, totaling $19.7 billion; RAYTHEON COMPANY appears four times, $18.5 billion — with its own corporate parent, RTX CORPORATION, listed as a separate record, the ninth-largest of the year at $7.3 billion. The mechanism is not a glitch. Each record is grouped by UEI — the SAM.gov registration that replaced the old DUNS number in 2022 — and one parent company can hold many UEIs, one per division, subsidiary, or legacy registration. The ranking adds up registrations, not corporations. Where a company registers under a single UEI, no splitting happens: several of the largest records appear only once in the 100 we read — Optum Public Sector Solutions ($22.5 billion, the second-largest record of the year), Electric Boat ($21.4 billion), TriWest Healthcare Alliance ($13.4 billion), McKesson ($11.9 billion), AmerisourceBergen ($7.4 billion) — each a single line here, though the slice cannot rule out other registrations below the cut. Four of those five are health-care and pharmaceutical firms; among the top 100, it is the defense primes — Lockheed, Boeing, Raytheon — that appear under the most identically-named records.
The Analysis
The following is analysis. The number a reader takes from the top of this ledger — the largest contractor got $34 billion — is not wrong, but it is not the company either. Counting only the records that carry Lockheed's name letter-for-letter nearly doubles it, to $59 billion, and lifts its share of all federal contract dollars from 4.4% to 7.6%. That 7.6% counts only the identical-name records within the 100 we read — not a full corporate-family total, which the raw ranking does not resolve. What the top-100 read shows is a mechanical feature of the ranking: it sums registrations, not corporations. One name spelled identically can hold eight lines because a single company can register many UEIs — one per division, subsidiary, or legacy registration. So the recipient field read at face value — a who-got-the-most list — treats those eight identical-name lines as eight recipients rather than one name. Whether that changes a full-ledger concentration estimate, and by how much, is not something a top-100 slice can settle: records outside it can carry negative modifications as well as positive obligations, so we do not claim these sums are a floor on any company's total. This is not concealment, and we assert no intent. USAspending groups by entity registration because that is what the procurement system records, and it offers parent-level rollups elsewhere, in its recipient profiles. But the default ranking — the one a reader lands on — sums registrations, and a name read once versus eight times is the difference between a company that is 4% of federal contracting and one that is 8% of it.
Room for Disagreement
The strongest counter is that the separate records are not duplicates. Eight distinct UEIs are eight real, separately registered legal entities — subsidiaries, divisions, joint ventures, post-merger registrations — and treating them as one parent is itself an editorial judgment, not a fact in the data. Our grouping is deliberately conservative: we merged only records whose names are spelled identically, which is unambiguous, and we did not fold in Sikorsky or other differently-named subsidiaries. USAspending is doing what it says — ranking recipients as the government registers them — and it publishes parent rollups in its recipient profiles for anyone who wants them. Two more limits. These are single-year obligation figures, which are lumpy; a multi-year view would move the exact shares, though not the fact of fragmentation. And we read the 100 largest records, not the entire recipient list, so our $59.0 and $64.7 billion figures describe only the identical-name records within that top 100 — not a full-ledger or parent-resolved total, which we did not compute.
The View From
From the vantage of the single top line — largest contractor, $34.1 billion — fiscal 2025 looks routine: Lockheed Martin leads, as it usually does, and the number is real. That reading is self-contained and it is also incomplete, because the same company is sitting seven more times further down the same list under the same name. It takes reading all 100 lines and matching the strings to see that the government's biggest supplier is not one entry near the top but eight entries scattered through the page — and that the tidy 4.4% at the top becomes 7.6% once the identical name is counted once, across the 100 lines we read.
