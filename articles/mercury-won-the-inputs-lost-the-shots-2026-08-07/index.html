The Analysis
The following is analysis, not fact. Phoenix out-attempted Connecticut by 11 field-goal tries and turned the ball over 8 fewer times, and still lost. Where the teams diverged was accuracy, not volume: both made exactly 27 field goals, Connecticut shooting 47.4% to Phoenix's 39.7%, and 35.0% to 17.9% from three. Phoenix funneled 41.2% of its 68 attempts — 28 shots — to the three-point line, and those returned 5. The 11-attempt edge did not sit on top of a clean possession advantage: Phoenix was out-rebounded 34-29, and Connecticut's own 18 turnovers (to Phoenix's 10) returned possessions the other way, so the extra shots came from a mixed ledger, not a single controllable input. A one-game shooting split does not, on its own, separate shot quality, selection, and defense from ordinary game-to-game swing — all of them sit inside a make rate — and this piece assigns the outcome to none of them.
Room for Disagreement
The comeback was not simply handed over; Connecticut earned parts of it. Its third-quarter defense held Phoenix to 9 points, its close-outs may have degraded some of those 28 three-point looks rather than merely watching them miss sspeculative, and the go-ahead basket was a made 24-footer inside three minutes, not a Phoenix mistake. A three-point make rate is never pure luck — it carries shot quality and defense inside it — so calling the loss "variance" overstates a clean line mmodeled. And a 75-72 result can be decomposed a dozen ways; the three-point column is one honest cut, not the only one — Connecticut's 6-2 edge in fast-break points and its 34-29 on the glass belong in the same ledger. The claim that survives the objections is the narrow, descriptive one: Phoenix out-attempted Connecticut and gave the ball away less, and lost on a night it shot 5-of-28 from three and 13-of-23 from the line.
