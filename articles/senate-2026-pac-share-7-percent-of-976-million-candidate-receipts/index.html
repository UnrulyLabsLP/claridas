We read every funded 2026 Senate campaign: PACs supplied 7% of the $978 million raised — individuals gave nearly six times as much, and 173 of 307 candidates took no PAC money at all

The candidate committees of the 307 active Senate contenders who have reported money to the FEC took in $977.6 million this cycle. Political-action committees account for $69.4 million of it — 7.1%. The picture inverts the popular one: the biggest war chests are among the least PAC-reliant, PAC dollars cluster on a handful of veteran incumbents, and none of it counts the unlimited super-PAC money that never touches a candidate's account.

Filed by the Claridas us pod · August 21, 2026 · Updated August 23, 2026

The Facts
Say "campaign finance" and most people picture political-action committees — organized interest money flowing to candidates. So here is the number that inverts the image. We pulled the Federal Election Commission's complete list of active 2026 Senate candidates and summed every committee's reported totals: 307 candidates have reported taking in money, and together their receipts run to $977,550,125 as of the Aug. 22 pull. Of that, PACs — non-party political committees — supplied $69.4 million. That is 7.1%. Itemized contributions from individuals were 40.5% of the total — 5.7 times the entire PAC share. Transfers a candidate moved between their own committees were another 16.7%; the remaining 35.7% is a mix the FEC's summary series does not split — unitemized individual contributions, party-committee money, and candidates' own loans. Even measured only against the individual money the FEC itemizes line by line, PACs are outweighed almost six to one. Read candidate by candidate, the median PAC share is zero. Of the 307, 173 took no PAC money at all; 272 raised more from individuals than from PACs; not one drew even half its money from PACs, and only 12 drew more than a quarter. Reliance on PACs runs opposite to fundraising success. The three biggest raisers are among the least PAC-reliant: Jon Ossoff (D-GA), $98.0 million at 1.1% PAC; James Talarico (D-TX), $68.6 million at 0.5%; Sherrod Brown (D-OH), $38.6 million at 2.2%. Among candidates who raised more than $5 million, the highest PAC shares belong to lower-profile incumbents of both parties — Shelley Moore Capito (R-WV) at 42.6% of $6.5 million, Roger Marshall (R-KS) at 40.4% of $6.2 million, Chris Coons (D-DE) at 32.2%, and Cindy Hyde-Smith (R-MS) at 27.0%. All told, the 26 incumbents in the field hold 68.4% of every PAC dollar. Three limits on the read. The figures are as candidates reported through filing periods ending mostly June 30, 2026 — the second-quarter reports, the latest posted. Senate totals accumulate over the full six-year cycle, so an incumbent's number runs from January 2021 while a challenger who launched in 2025 shows only months; that is why the composition share, not the raw dollar total, carries the finding — a share is computed within each candidate, which strips out the raw-dollar effect of window length, though the composition itself can still shift as a campaign matures. And the totals aggregate all of a candidate's authorized committees (the FEC's candidate-totals basis); counts stated on that basis, such as none drawing even half from PACs, shift on a principal-committee-only basis, so the share, not the exact rank, is the finding.
The Analysis
In the money that actually passes through a Senate candidate's own account, PACs are a small share, and the candidates who raise the most are the ones who lean on them least. The direct-PAC channel is capped at $5,000 per election for a multicandidate PAC — $3,500 for a nonmulticandidate committee — a ceiling that helps explain why direct-PAC money stays small next to individual giving, though the cap alone does not fix the share [verified — FEC contribution limits]. What the complete read adds is where PAC money matters proportionally most: to the lower-raising incumbents, and barely at all to the biggest raisers. Among these candidates the split tracks fundraising scale more than party — the low-PAC big raisers and the higher-PAC lower raisers both include Democrats and Republicans — though one field in one cycle cannot establish that as a law. A single quarterly filing cannot show even this much; one candidate's report is consistent with PACs being central or marginal. Only reading the whole active field at once fixes which it is. One thing this ledger does not establish sspeculative: that organized interest money is small in Senate races. It establishes that little of it moves through the candidates' own committees — a narrower claim, and the honest one.
Room for Disagreement
Two readings, and the gap between them is the argument. One: this is the democratization of campaign money. Individual donors now outweigh the PACs, and the 7.1% direct-PAC share is read as the evidence. The stronger objection: the low share is not evidence PACs are absent but evidence they moved. Because direct contributions are capped while independent expenditures are not, much organized money flows through super PACs and outside groups that never appear in a candidate's receipts — unlimited, and invisible in this dataset. On that reading, 7.1% understates interest-group influence rather than measuring it. The middle is arithmetic. The 7.1% is a clean, reproducible fact about candidate committees and nothing more. It settles the composition of one ledger; it says nothing about total spending in these races, which these filings do not contain. We report the delta, not a verdict on influence.
The View From
From any single campaign's press release the story is "$X raised, Y% from small donors." Read across all 307 committees at once and a pattern appears that no release states: in this field the direct-PAC channel is marginal for the candidates winning the money race, and a larger share of the money for the lower-raising incumbents.
