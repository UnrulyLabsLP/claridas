The Facts
Indiana beat Toronto 101–95 at Scotiabank Arena on Aug. 18, 2026, before 19,300 — the Fever's first trip to face the expansion Tempo in Toronto. We read the full box score, and by raw volume Toronto did more of almost everything. The Tempo took 87 shots to Indiana's 70 — 17 more attempts. They grabbed 18 offensive rebounds to Indiana's 7, nearly triple the visitor's total, and won the glass overall 41–32. They even made more three-pointers: 12 to Indiana's 11. And they turned it over less often, 11 giveaways to 15. Every one of those is a counting stat that reads, on a box score, like the winning team's line — more shots, more boards, more makes from deep, fewer turnovers. Toronto lost — and, per the play-by-play, never led for a single second of the 40 minutes. Indiana recorded zero lead changes, led an official 97% of game clock, and the Tempo's largest lead of the night was 0. The reason sits in the efficiency columns, not the volume ones: Indiana shot 53% from the field (37 of 70) and scored 50 of its points in the paint; Toronto shot 39% (34 of 87), took 37 of its 87 shots from behind the arc, and managed 32 in the paint. Re-derived from the final line, Indiana scored 1.44 points per field-goal attempt to Toronto's 1.09. Kelsey Mitchell scored 16 of her 29 in the first quarter before sitting the fourth — coach Stephanie White said she got "overheated" — and her 20-plus-point game was her 20th in a row, tying A'ja Wilson's WNBA record. Caitlin Clark added 24, Aliyah Boston 16, and Sophie Cunningham went 3-for-3 from three for 13. Marina Mabrey led Toronto with 22 on 7-of-21 shooting (5 of 14 from deep); Aneesah Morrow had 14 points and 16 rebounds; Brittney Sykes scored 15 in her return from injury. The loss was Toronto's 11th straight.
The Analysis
Strip the game to its quarters and a stranger story appears: Toronto outscored Indiana 72–68 across the second, third and fourth periods. The Tempo lost the game entirely in the first 10 minutes, when Indiana scored 33 to Toronto's 23 — 16 of those points off Mitchell's hand alone. Everything after was a comeback that ran out of court. That is the full-box-score read the final-line summary misses: this was not a team that got run off the floor, but a team that spotted a more efficient opponent a quarter and spent 30 minutes failing to make it back.

The 18 offensive rebounds are the tell. Offensive boards are not, on their own, a sign of a dominant night — they are what a cold-shooting team collects, because a missed shot is the precondition for an offensive rebound. mmodeled Toronto's 18 offensive rebounds and its 17-shot edge move with a 39% night: the extra boards and extra shots came alongside a cold-shooting game, in which more misses meant more loose balls to chase. Indiana, shooting 53%, rarely needed a second chance — it took the ball, scored in the paint, and walked back on defense. The three-point line makes the point again. Toronto made one more three than Indiana, which the box score presents as a Tempo advantage. Room for Disagreement
The strongest case against this framing is that it credits Toronto for numbers that are symptoms, not virtues. Seventeen extra shots and 18 offensive rebounds describe a team that missed a lot, not a team that outplayed anyone; a 53%-shooting opponent does not need to crash the glass because it does not miss. On that reading the "paradox" dissolves into the oldest result in basketball: the more efficient team, with the two best scorers on the floor — Mitchell's 29 and Clark's 24 on a combined 18-of-32 — won, and the volume columns are just the loser's fingerprints. mmodeled There is also a context caveat that cuts against reading too much into Toronto's late surge: Mitchell, the game's leading scorer, sat the entire fourth quarter after overheating, so Toronto's 26–20 edge in that period came with Indiana's leading scorer off the floor. And the matchup was lopsided on paper — an expansion team on an 11-game losing streak hosting a team on a five-game winning streak is not a coin flip. All true. The finding is not that Toronto deserved to win; it is that the box score's volume columns tell a story its efficiency columns flatly contradict, and only the full read reconciles them.
