WORLD

194 governments set a 90% child-vaccination floor to reach by 2020. In the target year the count of economies clearing it fell to 105 of 193 — and 2023 still trails 2019

We read WHO and UNICEF's DTP3 coverage estimates for every economy, every year since 2019. The backslide isn't only poor or war-torn states — nine high-income economies miss the mark, and on measles, the disease that needs near-95% coverage, barely half the world clears even 90 on the first dose.

Filed by the Claridas world pod · August 21, 2026

The Facts
In 2012, the 194 member states of the World Health Assembly endorsed the Global Vaccine Action Plan and, with it, a specific number: 90% national coverage for the third dose of the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis vaccine — DTP3, the standard yardstick for whether a country's routine immunization system works — reached by 2015 and sustained through 2020. We pulled WHO and UNICEF's coverage estimates for every economy the World Bank tracks, for each year from 2019 through 2023 (the World Bank series, drawn from the WHO/UNICEF estimates, last updated 2026-07-13). One hundred ninety-three economies — effectively every country the two agencies track — carry a DTP3 estimate in all five years. These are modeled estimates, not raw administrative tallies; the counts below are our arithmetic on that published series. Against the 90% line, the count of economies clearing it fell as the deadline arrived and has not climbed back. In 2019, 123 of the 193 were at or above 90%. In 2020 — the target year — 105 were. Then 103 in 2021, 108 in 2022, and 109 in 2023. The panel's median coverage slipped from 93% in 2019 to 91% and has stayed there. In 2023, 84 of the 193 economies sat below the 90% floor; 48 were below 80%, and three below 50% — Papua New Guinea (35%), the Central African Republic (40%) and Yemen (46%). The steepest multi-year declines since 2019 were in Dominica (99 to 56), Sudan (93 to 51), Lebanon (83 to 53), Vietnam (89 to 65) and Azerbaijan (74 to 51). The largest gain was Brazil's, up 20 points to exactly 90. Falling short is not only a poor- or conflict-country story. Nine high-income economies were below 90% in 2023: Panama (71%), Romania (79%), Estonia (84%), Barbados (85%), Austria and the Bahamas (87%), New Zealand (88%), and Germany and Slovenia (89%). Measles is starker. On the first measles dose in 2023, only 98 of the 193 economies reached even 90%, and just 64 reached 95% — the coverage the WHO and Gavi cite as the threshold to stop outbreaks.
The Analysis
The following is analysis, not fact. What the full panel adds, over the two or three countries a health desk usually names, is the shape of the miss. The count of economies clearing 90% did not drift down at random: it stepped down precisely as the 2020 deadline came, then held below its pre-pandemic level for three straight years. A single-year snapshot shows a world at "about 85% coverage"; the five-year read shows a target that arrived and passed with fewer countries meeting it than the year before. WHO attributes the 2020-21 fall to pandemic disruption of routine services, and several of the deepest declines are in states with wars or collapsing health systems — Sudan, Lebanon, Yemen. mmodeled We did not test that: no conflict, spending or health-system variable was joined to these counts, and no competing predictors were modeled. It is an association the panel is consistent with, not a mechanism these figures isolate. sspeculative The nine high-income laggards cut against the reflex that coverage is a money problem. Reaching 90% is partly about trust and delivery, not only budgets — and the panel's biggest single gainer, Brazil, is an upper-middle-income economy that climbed 20 points to the line while wealthier states slid under it. The measles gap carries the sharpest warning: national averages that clear a bar for DTP3 still fall well short of the near-95% that the most contagious vaccine-preventable disease requires. mmodeled
Room for Disagreement
The strongest counter is that 90% national coverage is the easy half of the pledge. The Global Vaccine Action Plan also asked for 80% coverage in every district — and far fewer countries meet that, because national averages hide the pockets where children go unreached. Counting only who clears the national line therefore flatters the record, not damns it. That is a fair objection, and it cuts against these numbers looking generous, not harsh. Second, these are WHO/UNICEF modeled estimates carrying real uncertainty; a country recorded at 89% versus 90% is inside the noise, so the exact count of economies sitting right at the threshold is sensitive to revision. The direction across five years is robust; the single-point tallies are less so. Third, DTP3 and first-dose measles are proxies for a functioning program, not a full childhood schedule — clearing them is necessary, not sufficient.
The View From
From Geneva, the agencies that set the 90% line now measure the gap by a different number: the "zero-dose" child who never gets a single shot. WHO and UNICEF's later 2024 revision counted 14.3 million such infants worldwide — more than in 2019 — and the successor plan, Immunization Agenda 2030, restates a 90% coverage goal for essential vaccines by decade's end. mmodeled The reframing is telling: a decade after pledging to reach 90% everywhere, the institutions track how many children reach zero.
