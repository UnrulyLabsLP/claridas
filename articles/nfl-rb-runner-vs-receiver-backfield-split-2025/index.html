We read all 32 NFL backfields: on a third, the back who runs the ball isn't the back who catches it

In 2025 the leading rusher and the leading pass-catcher were different players in 10 of 32 backfields, and 15 lead runners took under half their own backfield's catches — the passing down is a separately owned job the depth-chart 'starter' label hides.

Filed by the Claridas sports pod · August 24, 2026

The Facts
A single-team manager drafts a running back and reads one line: carries, yards, catches and touchdowns, all filed under one name. Read all 32 NFL backfields at once and the name splits apart. In nflverse's complete 2025 regular-season file — all 18 weeks, every team, each week's touches credited to the club the back actually played for that week — the running back who carried the ball most and the running back who caught it most were two different players on 10 of the 32 teams. Widen the lens and the divide is bigger than any depth chart admits. On 15 of 32 teams the leading ball-carrier took fewer than half of his own backfield's receptions. Baltimore is the sharpest case: Derrick Henry ran 307 times, one of the NFL's heaviest carry loads, and caught 15 passes — 27% of the Ravens' running-back receptions, behind Justice Hill's 21. Pittsburgh's Jaylen Warren led the team with 211 carries; Kenny Gainwell, nominally the backup, out-caught him 73 to 40 and turned it into 486 receiving yards and 3 receiving touchdowns. Tennessee (Tony Pollard runs, Tyjae Spears catches), Cleveland (Quinshon Judkins runs, Dylan Sampson catches) and Denver (J.K. Dobbins ran 153 times, RJ Harvey caught it 47 times to Dobbins' 11) split the job the same way. The other end of the pool is where the two jobs collapse back into one man. Christian McCaffrey ran for 311 of San Francisco's 403 running-back carries and caught 102 of its 110 running-back receptions — 92.7% of the catches. Bijan Robinson (84.9% of Atlanta's backfield receptions), De'Von Achane (84.8% in Miami), Jonathan Taylor (71.9% in Indianapolis) and rookie Ashton Jeanty (74.3% in Las Vegas) are the true every-down backs: they own the ground game and the passing down at once. They are the minority. Only 8 of 32 lead runners held even 70% of their backfield's carries, and the median lead runner held 60.5%.
The Analysis
The following is analysis, not fact. The opportunity-versus-output spine of this position has a hidden seam, and it runs between the carry and the catch. A carry and a target are each a form of usage that tends to recur week to week mmodeled, but they are assigned separately — a coach can hand one back the early-down grind and another the third-down route, and the box score still files both under 'the backfield'. In point-per-reception scoring, where a catch is worth a point before a single yard is gained, that seam is where fantasy value quietly changes hands. Pittsburgh's Gainwell is the clean illustration. A back most managers filed as a handcuff produced roughly 140 PPR points on receiving work alone — 73 catches, 486 yards, 3 scores — while the man ahead of him on the carry count wore the starter's label. The single-team manager sees the rushing total and the depth-chart tag; the whole-pool read shows the passing down sitting on a separate roster line on nearly a third of teams.
Room for Disagreement
A runner-catcher split is not automatically a committee, and not automatically new. Baltimore pairing a bell-cow grinder with a designated third-down back is a stable, decades-old design, not a fractured touch share — Henry still owned 78% of the carries. Some of the other divisions are churn, not scheme: Denver's Dobbins-Harvey arrangement reflects a mid-year backfield in flux more than a settled plan mmodeled. Share of receptions is a proxy, not a points ledger — receiving yards and touchdowns vary within it, so two backs at the same catch share can be worth very different totals. This also counts only running-back-coded players; targets that leak to fullbacks or players coded at other positions sit outside the frame (a back who lines up in the slot is still counted, as long as he is coded a running back). And it is a 2025 record: 2026 rosters have already moved rookies and free agents through these backfields, so nothing here forecasts next season's division of labor sspeculative.
The View From
From inside one roster you see your back's stat line and a starter's tag. From the read across all 32, the label thins out: on a third of teams the job that scores first in PPR — the catch — belongs to someone whose name isn't on the carries.
