WORLD

The UN's child-survival goal wrote two floors, not one. Read all 196 economies' 2023 estimates and the newborn floor is the one that traps the near-winners — 9 clear the under-5 line but miss it, to just 3 the other way

120 economies clear both the 25-per-1,000 under-5 threshold and the 12-per-1,000 neonatal one; 64 clear neither. But of the dozen that split, every one of the nine still short on newborns has already beaten the child floor — the first 28 days is the harder half.

Filed by the Claridas world pod · August 25, 2026

The Facts

Sustainable Development Goal target 3.2 does not set one deadline for keeping children alive. It sets two numbers. By 2030, the UN framework asks every country to cut its under-5 mortality to at least as low as 25 deaths per 1,000 live births, and — separately — its neonatal mortality, deaths in the first 28 days, to at least as low as 12 per 1,000. A country can clear one line and miss the other. We pulled both series the World Bank mirrors from the UN Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation — SH.DYN.MORT for under-5 and SH.DYN.NMRT for neonatal, data year 2023, updated 13 July 2026 — and kept every economy with a 2023 value for both. That is 196 of the World Bank's 217 non-aggregate economies; 21 carry no 2023 figure for one series or the other. These are modeled estimates, blending surveys, censuses and vital registration because most countries cannot count either rate directly. mmodeled Scored against the two fixed lines: 129 of the 196 clear the under-5 floor, and 123 clear the neonatal floor. But the counts hide the pairing. 120 economies clear both; 64 clear neither. Only 12 sit on opposite sides of the two lines — and they split 9 to 3. Nine clear the under-5 floor yet miss the newborn one; just three do the reverse. The nine are not poor countries. None is low-income: Moldova (neonatal 12.1), Grenada (12.5), Azerbaijan and Iraq (12.9), Trinidad and Tobago (13.0), Jamaica (13.9), Egypt (14.2), Venezuela (15.0) and Algeria (15.3) — all already under the 25 line on under-5 deaths, all stalled just above 12 on newborns. The three going the other way, clearing the newborn floor while missing the child one, are West Bank and Gaza (under-5 41.9, neonatal 9.2), Libya (31.7 and 5.8) and Tajikistan (28.8 and 11.3). Across all 196 the median economy sits at 15.4 under-5 and 8.5 neonatal. mmodeled

The Analysis

The following is analysis, not fact. The vantage most desks take on child mortality is the global rate — UN IGME put under-5 at 37.4 per 1,000 and neonatal at 17.2 in 2024 — or the two regions, sub-Saharan Africa and southern Asia, that carry most of the deaths. Read every economy's two filings side by side and a different question answers itself: which of the two floors is the operative one for the countries almost across the line. For 184 of the 196 the two floors agree — a country either clears both or neither, and the second number tells you nothing the first didn't. The split shows up only at the margin, in the 12 that clear exactly one line, and there it is directional: 9 to 3, the newborn floor is the one still unmet. What makes the nine cohere is that all of them have already cleared the harder-sounding, whole-childhood floor. They have driven down the deaths that come after the first month — the pneumonia, diarrhoea and vaccine-preventable disease of later infancy — and are held back only by the deaths clustered in the first 28 days. Points to, not proves, a last-mile shape: once a country fixes older-baby survival, the newborn floor is what remains, and it moves last. We did not model this — no health-system, coverage or spending variable was joined — so read it as a pattern the ledger is consistent with, not a mechanism these two columns isolate. sspeculative The reverse three make the same point from the other side. Their newborn rates are low — Libya's 5.8 sits well below its North African neighbours, Egypt's 14.2 and Algeria's 15.3 — while their under-5 rates run high, the signature of older-child deaths from causes a delivery room does not touch. The newborn floor, on this read, is not the poor-country floor. It is the closing floor. sspeculative

Room for Disagreement

Twelve of 196 is a thin margin to hang a shape on. For 184 economies the two floors move together, so calling the newborn line the binding one describes the edge of the distribution, not its center — most of the world is failing or clearing both at once, and there the first number is the whole story. And the split runs through the noise band. UN IGME publishes wide uncertainty intervals, and the fractions separating these near-winners from 12 are often smaller than those intervals: Moldova at 12.1 is a rounding revision away from clearing, Grenada at 12.5 likewise. A future vintage could move several of the nine across the line in either direction, so the 9-to-3 count is a snapshot of one modeled release, not a fixed fact. The two low-income economies that clear both floors here — Syria and North Korea — are a reminder that modeled estimates in data-poor states carry the least ground truth. Finally, both lines are minimums: SDG 3.2's actual goal is ending preventable newborn and child deaths, and clearing 25 and 12 is the floor of that ambition, not its finish. mmodeled

The View From

**View from a near-target health ministry.** To a public-health office in Chisinau, Kingston or Cairo, the asymmetry is not a mystery — it is a budget line. The deaths after the first month yield to things that scale cheaply and fast: vaccines, oral rehydration, primary clinics. The deaths in the first 28 days yield to delivery-room capacity, trained birth attendants, neonatal intensive care and referral that works at 3 a.m. — the slowest and most expensive part of a health system to build. Clearing the under-5 floor first, and the newborn floor last, is what that cost curve looks like from the inside. This is our reading of the mechanism, not one these mortality figures isolate. sspeculative
