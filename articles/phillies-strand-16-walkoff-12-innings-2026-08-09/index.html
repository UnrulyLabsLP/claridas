Philadelphia reached base 21 times, stranded 16, and needed 12 innings to beat Toronto by one

Both teams managed exactly 10 hits. The Phillies drew 11 walks to the Blue Jays' 3, blew a 5-2 lead, fell behind again in the 12th — and won on a walk-off only after burying 16 runners on the bases.

The Facts
On 9 August 2026 the Philadelphia Phillies beat the Toronto Blue Jays 7-6 in 12 innings at Citizens Bank Park, on a walk-off, before an announced 40,398. The two lineups finished with the same hit total: 10 apiece. Everywhere else the ledger favored the team that nearly lost. Philadelphia drew 11 walks to Toronto's 3, so it reached base 21 times by hit or walk to Toronto's 13. It also left 16 runners on base to Toronto's 9 — the official team totals on the MLB linescore. Philadelphia struck out 9 times; Toronto struck out 15. The Phillies led 5-2 after six innings, with Kyle Schwarber accounting for the efficient runs — a three-run homer in the third (his 34th) and a solo shot in the fifth (his 35th). Then the traffic stopped converting. Toronto tied it 5-5 in the top of the eighth on an Andrés Giménez two-run single. The ninth went scoreless, then so did the tenth and eleventh despite the automatic runner each extra half-inning places on second base starting in the tenth. In the top of the twelfth Toronto pushed its free runner home for a 6-5 lead. Philadelphia answered in the bottom half: a Bryson Stott single scored Luis Arraez to tie it, and a Derek Hill single scored J.T. Realmuto to end it, 7-6. The Analysis
The box score reads 7-6, a one-run thriller. Read the whole record at once and the margin looks less like a coin flip than a team surviving its own volume. Philadelphia put 21 men on base by hit or walk and scored 7 of them; a 21-baserunner night that yields only 7 runs is not, on its own, a story of clutch failure — with runners in scoring position the Phillies hit roughly average by our count of the play-by-play, about 5 for 12 mmodeled — it is a story of sheer traffic. When a lineup reaches base 21 times and adds three more free runners in the tenth, eleventh and twelfth, 16 left on base is closer to arithmetic than collapse: there were simply more runners than any normal conversion rate could clear [verified/modeled]. The 11 walks are the engine. Toronto's pitching handed Philadelphia a baserunner nearly every inning without a ball in play, which is why the Phillies could strand 16, blow a three-run lead, fall behind in the twelfth, and still win — they kept refilling the bases faster than they emptied them. The strikeout column points the same way: Toronto whiffed 15 times to Philadelphia's 9, so the Blue Jays' 10 hits arrived with less surrounding traffic and cashed at a higher rate — 6 runs on 13 baserunners against 7 on 21. Two teams, ten hits each; the difference was that one of them also drew eight more walks and needed all of them, plus twelve innings, to win by a run.
Room for Disagreement
This is one game, and much of what makes 16 stranded runners look extreme is the format, not a failing. The game went 12 innings, so both sides batted far more than a regulation nine, and Major League Baseball's automatic-runner rule places a free man on second to open each extra half-inning — three of Philadelphia's stranded innings came with a runner it did not earn. Normalize for length and the strand total is high but not historic; the single-game team record sits around 20 mmodeled. A fair reading is that walks-heavy, extra-innings games simply generate leftover runners, and Philadelphia's was an ordinary if untidy win. The win-probability figures are a model, not a measurement: the 33% floor and the 0.474 swing come from ESPN's estimator and would move under a different one mmodeled. What is not in dispute is the shape of the ledger: same hits, an 11-to-3 edge in walks, 16 runners left to 9, and a one-run result that took twelve innings to reach.

What a human would miss
Watch the ninth-inning-and-beyond highlights and you get a walk-off: two clutch singles in the twelfth, a one-run win, a happy crowd. Read the whole sheet at once and the win looks less earned than survived. Philadelphia reached base 21 times, more than enough traffic to win two or three ordinary games, and squeezed only 7 runs out of it — then had to answer a go-ahead run in the twelfth to avoid losing a game in which it had out-reached its opponent by eight baserunners. The detail a box score won't flag is that the 16 stranded runners are not a choke; the Phillies hit about average with men in scoring position. They simply drew 11 walks and kept putting men on faster than they could drive them in, so the leftovers piled up until, in the twelfth, the 21st baserunner of the night finally came home. The number nobody reads off a walk-off is how close a team can come to wasting an eight-runner advantage — and still need the last at-bat to keep it.
