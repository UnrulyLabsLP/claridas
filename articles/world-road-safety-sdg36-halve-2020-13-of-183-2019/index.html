WORLD

One UN road-safety target fell due in 2020, not 2030 — halve road deaths. We read WHO's rate estimates for the 183 economies it models: 13 halved their road-death rate, 71 got worse, and the global rate fell 8%

SDG target 3.6 set a rare 2020 deadline. Measured 2010 to 2019, the deepest cuts were concentrated in Gulf and Central Asian economies that started with high death rates — while Western Europe, generally starting far lower, largely did not halve, and the United States moved the wrong way, 11.7 to 12.7 per 100,000.

Filed by the Claridas world pod · August 26, 2026

The Facts
In 2015 every UN member state adopted Sustainable Development Goal 3.6: 'By 2020, halve the number of global deaths and injuries from road traffic accidents.' It is one of the few SDG targets that came due in 2020, not 2030. The target was not met; in 2020 the UN General Assembly proclaimed a new 2021-2030 Decade of Action, with a goal of reducing road-traffic deaths and injuries by at least 50% (resolution A/RES/74/299) — the horizon the World Health Organization now cites. We pulled WHO's modeled road-traffic mortality rate — deaths per 100,000 people — from the World Bank series SH.STA.TRAF.P5 (source: WHO; last updated 2026-07-13; latest modeled year 2019) for every economy carrying a value at both the 2010 baseline and 2019, the last full year WHO modeled before the deadline. 183 of the world's 217 economies qualify. Across those 183, the global rate fell from 18.2 to 16.7 deaths per 100,000 — an 8.0% drop, about one-sixth of the way to halving. Thirteen economies actually halved their rate. Another 98 fell by less than half, 71 rose, and one held level; the median economy cut its rate 7.7%. The deep cuts came from economies that began the decade with very high death rates. Oman went from 27.8 to 10.6, Kazakhstan 25.9 to 12.7, Belarus 21.9 to 7.6, the United Arab Emirates 19.4 to 8.9 — joined by Qatar, Bahrain, Trinidad and Tobago and Montenegro, all economies whose 2010 rates ranked among the highest on record. Eight of the thirteen halvers were high-income; only one was lower-middle income, and none were low-income.
The Analysis
The following is analysis, not fact. The pledge reads as a universal ambition; the record reads as a story about starting points. The economies that halved were mostly the ones with the most distance to fall — high-rate Gulf and post-Soviet states cutting from double-digit tolls. Western Europe and Japan, generally starting from much lower rates, largely did not halve: the United Kingdom moved 3.7 to 3.2, Germany 4.8 to 3.8, Japan 5.3 to 3.6, France 6.5 to 5.1, while Sweden and the Netherlands barely moved. Not one of them halved. The vantage a single national desk structurally cannot see is direction across the whole set: 71 economies — more than a third of the 183 — ended the decade worse than they began it. The United States is a prominent rich-country exception in the wrong direction, its rate rising from 11.7 to 12.7 — already several times its major European peers' and still climbing. The largest wrong-way move on the board is the Dominican Republic, from 27.8 to 64.6, and Saudi Arabia, a high-income state, rose 45%. Why any one country moved as it did is not something these figures isolate — the ranking is the finding, not a mechanism these data test. sspeculative
Room for Disagreement
Two cautions. First, metric: the SDG target counts deaths; we measured the per-100,000 rate, the standard cross-country comparator. The two diverge because population and traffic grew underneath the rate. WHO's own count-based tally has annual road deaths falling 5%, to 1.19 million in 2021, and names 10 countries — not our 13 — that cut the number of deaths by at least half; its endpoint is 2021 and ours 2019, its metric the count and ours the rate. By either yardstick the 2020 target was missed. Second, these are modeled estimates with wide uncertainty bands: the tiny-island swings (Tonga up more than fourfold, Micronesia to near zero) sit inside the noise, and the clearest large moves in the point estimates include the Gulf and Central Asian halvers, the US and Dominican Republic risers, though those estimates carry uncertainty too. sspeculative
The View From
**View from WHO:** the tally is heading downward but nowhere near fast enough, in the words of its director-general; the agency frames road crashes as the leading killer of people aged 5–29 and notes 92% of deaths fall in low- and middle-income countries — and it has folded the missed 2020 goal into a 2030 one. mmodeled **View from a high-income capital:** a rate already among the world's lowest leaves little left to halve, and a plateau near 3–5 per 100,000 can reflect a road system near its practical floor rather than complacency. sspeculative The reading that resists this defense is the United States, whose rate rose from an already-high base while its peers held or fell.
