Preseason football scored 18% fewer points than a regular-season week — not sloppier, just shorter of the red zone

We read every point and every drive of the 16 games played Aug 13–16 against a regular-season opening week: the same possessions, the same red-zone conversion, the same turnover rate. The only real gap was how often a drive reached scoring range.

Filed by the Claridas sports pod · August 21, 2026

The Facts

Preseason football carries a reputation: vanilla playbooks, starters benched by the second quarter, sloppy and beside the point. So we read all of it — every point, every drive, every red-zone trip across the 16 games played August 13–16, one for each of the NFL's 32 teams — and set the same read beside a real regular-season opening week (the 2025 season's Week 1, also 16 games, pulled the identical way). The scoreboards do differ. The preseason slate produced 542 points, 33.9 a game between both teams; the regular-season week produced 661, or 41.3 a game — 18% fewer. Two teams never reached the end zone at all: Kansas City's 12 points came on four field goals, Green Bay's nine on three. Underneath the scoreboard, though, it reads like ordinary football. The two weeks ran almost the same number of drives — 20.8 a game in the preseason, 20.2 in the regular season. They gave the ball away at nearly the same rate: 2.06 turnovers a game to 1.88, the preseason marginally higher, not lower. And when a preseason drive reached the red zone, it came away with a touchdown 55.3% of the time (47 of 85 trips) — indistinguishable from the regular-season week's 54.0% (54 of 100). Even the scoring mix matched: field goals were 28.8% of all preseason points and 27.2% of regular-season points, and touchdowns outnumbered made field goals by a slim margin both times — 55 to 52 in the preseason, 70 to 60 in the regular season. One number moved. Preseason drives reached the red zone 5.3 times a game; regular-season drives, 6.2 — about 15% fewer arrivals at the doorstep. Same possessions, same finishing, same care with the ball — just fewer trips deep enough to score.

The Analysis

The following is analysis, not fact. Preseason football's caricature is broken football: backups who can't execute, giveaways, chaos. The complete record of these 16 games points the other way mmodeled. The measures that capture execution once a team is in position — red-zone conversion, turnover rate, the touchdown-to-field-goal split — landed right on top of the regular-season baseline. What separated the two weeks was almost entirely how often a drive got deep enough to matter, and that gap of roughly 15% fewer red-zone trips tracks most of the 18% scoring gap mmodeled. That is a narrower claim than "the backups are worse." It says the offenses reached scoring range less often — a field-position and drive-sustaining problem, the part of the game most exposed to a rebuilt offensive line and an unfamiliar quarterback — but once they got there, third-stringers finished at a starter's rate. The sloppiness the preseason is known for didn't surface in the giveaway column or inside the 20. It surfaced as drives that stalled a little earlier.

Room for Disagreement

Two 16-game weeks are a thin sample, and the comparison leans on a Week 1 — itself the rustiest, lowest-scoring slice of the regular season, which narrows the very gap measured here mmodeled. Set the preseason beside a midseason Sunday and the point totals would likely diverge more. Aggregates also hide who was on the field: a red-zone touchdown converted by fourth-stringers against fourth-stringers is not the same event as a starters' snap, even when both count as seven mmodeled. And "reached the red zone less" is a description, not a cause — whether it traces to worse blocking, conservative play-calling, or simply shorter starter shifts is more than two box-score populations can settle sspeculative.

The View From

On a broadcast, a stalled preseason drive looks like a hopeful nobody blowing his audition. Read across all 16 games at once, the nobodies converted the red zone about as often as the Week 1 starters did. They just reached it less often.
