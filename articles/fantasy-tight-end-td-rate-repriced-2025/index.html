We read all 39 tight ends: 2025's fantasy TE order was set by touchdown rate — the one input that regresses

Re-score every qualified tight end at the position's own scoring rate and the board shuffles up to five spots: Dallas Goedert's TE7 finish, built on 591 yards and 11 touchdowns, falls to TE12 — while Juwan Johnson's 889 yards climb from TE8 to TE5.

Filed by the Claridas sports pod · August 19, 2026

The Facts
We pulled the complete 2025 regular-season stat line for every NFL tight end and kept the 39 who drew 40 or more targets — the qualifying pool [verified, nflverse `stats_player_reg_2025`]. Across those 39, tight ends caught 169 receiving touchdowns on 2,838 targets: a pooled rate of 5.95% of targets turned into a score [verified, re-derived]. The individual rates did not cluster around that mean. They ran from 1.2% (Cade Otton, 1 touchdown on 81 targets) to 14.3% (Colby Parkinson, 8 on 56) — an 11.6-fold spread on the single most variance-driven input to a tight end's fantasy total. That spread, not usage, sorted the top of the board. Dallas Goedert finished as the PPR TE7 (185.1 points) on 591 receiving yards in 15 games — 13 of the 39 qualified tight ends out-gained him. What carried him was 11 touchdowns on 82 targets, a 13.4% rate, third-highest among qualifiers and 2.25 times the pool average. At the pool's own 5.95% rate, 82 targets project to 4.9 touchdowns, not 11 — a gap of roughly six scores, or about 37 PPR points mmodeled. Re-score every qualified tight end at that neutral rate and Goedert's line falls to 148.4 points, which ranks TE12 in the re-priced pool — a five-spot drop mmodeled. The same math lifts the tight ends the scoreboard underpaid. Juwan Johnson gained 889 yards — third-most among the 39 — on 3 touchdowns (a 2.9% rate) and finished PPR TE8; re-priced to the pool rate he ranks TE5 mmodeled. Dalton Schultz (777 yards, 3 touchdowns, actual TE10) and rookie Tyler Warren (817 yards, 4 touchdowns, TE4) re-price to TE6 and TE3 respectively mmodeled. The one line the exercise barely moves is the one on top: Trey McBride's 126 catches, 1,239 yards and 11 touchdowns came on 169 targets — a 6.5% touchdown rate, right at the pool average — so his TE1 finish (315.9 points) holds at TE1 when re-priced (310.3) mmodeled. McBride and Goedert scored the same 11 touchdowns; McBride needed twice the targets to do it, and only one of the two finishes is a volume story.
The Analysis
The following is analysis, not fact. The "re-priced" board is a benchmark, not a forecast: it asks what each tight end's total would have been at the pool's average touchdown rate, holding targets and yards fixed mmodeled. It is a way to separate the repeatable part of a fantasy finish (targets, catches, yards — the workload) from the part that swings hardest year to year (how often that workload reached the end zone). Tight end is the position where that separation matters most, because the volume is thin: a tight end drawing 80–100 targets is a busy one, so three or four extra touchdowns — a single quarter of red-zone luck across a season — moves a player four or five spots on a 12-team board. At wide receiver, where the target counts run higher, the same touchdown swing is diluted across far more opportunity. Read that way, 2025's tight end results split cleanly into two groups the box score blends together. The finishers whose ranking rests on yardage and targets — McBride, Pitts (928 yards on the position's second-most targets, 118), Warren, Kelce (851 yards) — are describing a workload. The finishers whose ranking rests on an above-pool touchdown rate — Goedert at 13.4%, Parkinson at 14.3%, Tucker Kraft at 13.6% on 44 targets — are describing a rate, and rates this far above 5.95% are the first thing a full-season read expects to move mmodeled. Goedert is the sharpest case in the pool: 11 touchdowns on just 82 targets, a 13.4% rate the workload alone did not predict. The workload said role player; the scoreboard said TE7.
Room for Disagreement
A pool-average touchdown rate is a blunt neutral point, and the honest objection is that touchdown rate is not pure noise. Some tight ends earn a higher rate through a genuine red-zone role — size, target quality inside the 10, an offense that funnels goal-line looks to them. Goedert is exactly that argument: the Eagles' short-field offense and his own red-zone usage make his rate partly structural, not purely lucky, and reporting on his season called him "an integral red zone threat" mmodeled. Re-pricing him to the pool average almost certainly overcorrects; the true regression sits somewhere between 11 touchdowns and the 4.9 the flat model implies. We did not pull per-player red-zone target data this run, so the size of that structural share is unquantified here sspeculative. The reverse caveat applies to the underpaid group: a low touchdown rate can also foreshadow a smaller role, not a rebound. Johnson's 889-yard line came on heavy volume, and independent 2026 coverage flags an expected New Orleans role change — his yardage base may shrink even if his touchdown rate normalizes mmodeled. And this is a single completed season (2025); it is a look at what already happened, not a projection of 2026, and touchdown rates regress toward a moving mean, not a fixed one. The claim here is narrow and backward-looking: across these 39 tight ends, the fantasy order and the usage order diverged, and the wedge between them was a touchdown rate that ranged 12-fold around its own average.
The View From
From a single manager's chair, Goedert's TE7 line and Johnson's TE8 line look one slot apart. Read across all 39 qualified tight ends at once, they are the two ends of the position's central noise: one finish built on the yards, the other on the touchdowns that produced them at less than half Goedert's rate.
