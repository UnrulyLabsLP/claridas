We read every completed singles match at the Cincinnati Open: 9 winners took fewer games than the player they beat

Across 204 finished singles matches at the 2026 tournament, 9 were won by the player who won fewer total games — Alex de Minaur among them — and 8 more, including Novak Djokovic's loss, ended with both players winning exactly the same number. Tennis is built to throw away the margin inside a set, and the complete read shows how often that flips the scoreboard.

Filed by the Claridas sports pod · August 17, 2026

The Facts
We read every men's and women's singles match the 2026 Cincinnati Open had finished — all of them, not a highlight reel — and counted, for each one, how many games each player actually won. As of 21:01 America/New_York on 17 August 2026, ESPN's tournament scoreboard listed 212 completed singles matches; 204 were clean, full-length results (103 men's, 101 women's), and we set aside 8 (retirements or a walkover). That is the population: every completed singles match on the board, qualifying and main-draw rounds alike, frozen to one capture so every figure below is re-derivable from it. In 187 of those 204 matches, the winner also won more total games than the loser — the ordinary case. But in 9, the player who won the match won *fewer* games than the player they beat, and in 8 more the two finished with the exact same number of games. So in 17 of 204 completed singles matches — a little over 8% — winning more of the tennis did not win the match. The names are not obscure. Alex de Minaur beat Quentin Halys 1-6, 6-3, 7-6 while winning 14 games to Halys's 15. Kei Nishikori won 14 games to Francisco Comesana's 15 and advanced 6-4, 1-6, 7-5; Jakub Mensik (16 to 17), Arthur Fery (15 to 16), Wu Yibing (15 to 17 over Mackenzie McDonald) and Dusan Lajovic (16 to 18 over Benjamin Bonzi) all did the same. On the women's side Katie Boulter beat Katie Volynets 7-5, 0-6, 6-4 despite winning 13 games to 15; Clara Tauson won 15 to Peyton Stearns's 17; and Tamara Korpatsch lost the opening set to Maya Joint 0-6 — zero games — then won the match 12 games to 13. The 8 dead-even matches include marquee exits: Thiago Tirante beat Novak Djokovic 2-6, 6-4, 6-4 with each man winning 14 games, and Zeynep Sonmez beat Daria Kasatkina 2-6, 7-6, 6-3 with each winning 15. Nearly all share a shape: one lopsided set the eventual winner conceded cheaply — a 1-6, a 2-6, a 0-6 — offset by tighter sets elsewhere. Those tighter sets are often 7-6s and 7-5s, but not always: Tirante beat Djokovic on two 6-4s, and Korpatsch answered a 0-6 with a 6-3 and a 6-4.
The Analysis
Tennis is one of the few scored games where the units do not add up. Points nest inside games, games inside sets, sets inside the match, and at each seam the running margin is discarded: a set won 6-0 counts once, exactly as much as a set won 7-6. Win one set in a blowout and lose two in tiebreakers, and you can pile up games — and points behind them — while losing the only tally that advances you. The 8% gap we found is not noise in the data; it is the scoring system working as designed, made visible only by reading the whole draw at once. What the complete read fixes is the size of the effect. Total games is a very good proxy for who won — it agreed with the result 187 times in 204, about 92% — which is exactly why the broadcast instinct to trust "who's winning more" is usually right, and why the exceptions feel like glitches mmodeled. But the exceptions are structural, not flukes: each of the 9 reversals and 8 ties traces to a single conceded set — the cheapest thing to lose in tennis. De Minaur's 1-6 opening set cost him five games he never needed; Korpatsch's 0-6 cost her six, and neither mattered because a set is a set. The point is not that the wrong players advanced — the winners won the units that count, cleanly . It is that "who played better," measured the way most sports would measure it, and "who won," measured the way tennis does, come apart far more often than a single scoreline suggests — 17 times in one week of one tournament. A viewer who caught any one of these matches saw a result; the full board shows a pattern the sport builds in on purpose.
Room for Disagreement
A purist will say there is no paradox here at all, and the objection is fair: games are not tennis's currency, sets are, and by the rules the player who wins two sets has simply won more of what matters mmodeled. "Fewer games" is a lens the sport deliberately does not use, so calling it a reversal imports a scoring logic from other games mmodeled. We frame it as a divergence between two reasonable scoreboards, not an injustice. The buckets are also small — 9 and 8 — and this is a snapshot of an event still in progress, not the finished tournament; the population mixes qualifying and main-draw rounds, and a different slice of days would move the counts. We scoped every figure to exactly the 204 completed matches we captured and excluded the 8 non-final results (retirements and a walkover), rather than sell a running total as the whole event. Finally, games are themselves only one layer. Points would tell a third story — a player can win more games but fewer points, or the reverse — and the free source we pulled does not publish total points won, so we could not test that seam this run. What we can say holds: read end to end, the 2026 Cincinnati Open produced 17 singles matches in which the winner did not win more games than the player across the net.
The View From
From a single televised match, a reversal looks like a robbery — the player who "outplayed" the other somehow lost. From the whole draw, it looks like arithmetic: 17 of 204 times, tennis charged full price for a set and gave a discount on the games inside it. Neither view is wrong; only the second one counts.
