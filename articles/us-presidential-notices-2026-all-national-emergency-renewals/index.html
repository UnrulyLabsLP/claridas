Every one of the 30 presidential notices the Federal Register published in 2026 does the same thing: renew a national emergency — the oldest for a 31st year

Under a 1976 law meant to end open-ended emergencies, each one lapses on its anniversary unless the president publishes a continuation notice. We read all 30 published between Jan. 14 and Aug. 14: every notice is a renewal, the emergencies behind them were declared between 1995 and 2025, and 26 of the 30 rest on the same sanctions statute.

Filed by the Claridas us pod · August 22, 2026 · Updated August 22, 2026

The Facts
In 1976 Congress passed the National Emergencies Act to stop presidents from leaving emergencies open forever. Under 50 U.S.C. §1601 the Act terminated, two years after it took effect, the powers and authorities that had flowed from the emergencies then in force — the oldest dating to a 1933 banking crisis — subject to savings provisions, rather than erasing the declarations themselves. It also set an automatic clock: a national emergency now ends one year after it is declared unless, within the 90 days before that anniversary, the president publishes a notice of continuation in the Federal Register and tells Congress (50 U.S.C. 1622(d)). That renewal notice is a distinct document type in the Federal Register. We pulled every presidential document the Register published under the "notice" type in 2026 — 30 of them, published between Jan. 14 and Aug. 14. Every one is a continuation notice, titled "Continuation of the National Emergency" — the bundled border-and-cartels notice uses the plural, "National Emergencies." Not one does anything but renew. Reading each notice's text, the emergencies they extend were first declared across three decades — from 1995 to 2025. The oldest is the Iran sanctions emergency, Executive Order 12957 of March 15, 1995, renewed here for a 31st year. One 2026 notice continues more than one emergency, so the 30 notices carry at least 31 distinct emergencies; by origin decade they run two in the 1990s, six in the 2000s, eleven in the 2010s and twelve in the 2020s. The median origin year is about 2015, roughly a decade back; seventeen of the emergencies are at least ten years old, and six at least twenty. Twenty-six of the 30 notices cite the International Emergency Economic Powers Act by name — the 1977 statute that converts an emergency declaration into authority to impose sanctions and freeze assets. At the other end, six emergencies came up for their first renewal, having been declared in 2025: energy, the southern border, cartels, the International Criminal Court, tariff-related "trade practices," and Brazil. The full total is genuinely ambiguous at the top end: three February 2025 orders on Canada, Mexico and China read in the source as either new emergencies or expansions of an existing tariff emergency, so the active count these 30 notices imply runs from at least 31 up to about 34 — an ambiguity the Federal Register text does not resolve. One scope note. This is a partial year: the 30 are only the emergencies whose anniversaries fell between mid-January and mid-August. Emergencies with later anniversaries — including the one most often called the country's oldest, the Nov. 14, 1979 order blocking Iranian government property (Executive Order 12170) — renew after this window and are not among the 30. The Analysis
Any single notice is unremarkable — a one-page filing that says a threat "has not been resolved" and the emergency will continue. Reading all 30 at once shows what one filing cannot: the Federal Register's presidential-notice category, for the first seven and a half months of 2026, did exactly one job — keep existing emergencies from lapsing. That is the mechanism the 1976 law built, seen at full stretch. The Act was written to end perpetual emergencies; the record shows the annual clock it created being reset on schedule each time one comes due. The oldest declaration in this batch has now cleared that bar 31 times. What the whole set surfaces, and no single notice does, is the age ladder: an emergency declared last year and one declared in 1995 arrive at the Register in the same month, in the same format, and are handled identically. The renewal is routine by design — a signature, a paragraph, a filing — whatever the age of the threat underneath it.
Room for Disagreement
A renewal is not proof of inertia. Letting an IEEPA emergency lapse ends the authority to take new actions under it, though 50 U.S.C. §1706 lets specified property prohibitions continue even after an emergency ends, provided the president makes the determination the statute requires — so a lapse need not instantly unwind every blocked asset and designation. Either way, an annual continuation can be a live policy decision with real stakes, not a rubber stamp sspeculative. The count records that each emergency was renewed; it cannot show whether the underlying threat still warrants it, and reformers who want fixed sunsets and defenders who note the programs are actively enforced are arguing over the same 30 filings, not different ones. The scope also bounds the claim. These are the anniversaries that fell in one seven-month window, not the full active roster, and "the oldest here is 31 years" is not "the oldest emergency in force" — that one renews later in the year and is not in this pull. And "26 of 30 cite IEEPA" is a fact about the notices' text; a few emergencies rest on other authority, and at least one IEEPA-based order (Venezuela) is renewed without naming the statute in its notice.
