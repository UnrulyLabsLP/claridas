The Wings scored 23 more points than Minnesota on everything that wasn't a three. Kayla McBride went 10-for-14 from behind the arc — a new WNBA single-game record — and the three-point column alone was a 36-point swing.

The Facts
On 9 August 2026 the Minnesota Lynx beat the Dallas Wings 103-90 at Target Center. Dallas was the more efficient team almost everywhere the box score usually rewards: it shot 54% from the field to Minnesota's 56% but took fewer shots (37-of-68 to 40-of-72), scored 44 points in the paint to Minnesota's 34, went 10-of-11 from the free-throw line (91%) to Minnesota's 5-of-9 (56%), and out-rebounded the Lynx 34-24. It led 23-21 after the first quarter and by as many as eight. It still lost by 13, and the reason is a single column. Minnesota made 18 three-pointers to Dallas's 6, on 39 attempts to 20. That is 54 points from distance to 18 — a 36-point edge from behind the arc. On everything else — two-point field goals plus free throws — Dallas outscored Minnesota 72 to 49, a 23-point advantage. The two numbers close the game exactly: Minnesota's +36 from three minus Dallas's +23 on the rest equals the 13-point final margin. Minnesota's night from distance had one engine. Kayla McBride made 10 of 14 three-pointers for a career-high 43 points; the 10 threes broke the WNBA single-game record of nine, which per Basketball-Reference had been shared by six players — Marina Mabrey, Chelsea Gray, Rhyne Howard, Arike Ogunbowale, Jewell Loyd, and Kelsey Mitchell — and Ogunbowale was on the floor for Dallas, scoring 5. Olivia Miles (5-of-8) and Napheesa Collier (3-of-5) hit the other eight. Dallas got 23 from Paige Bueckers and 19 from Aziaha James but turned the ball over 18 times to Minnesota's 11, and the Lynx recorded 12 steals to Dallas's 5.
The Analysis
Strip the box score to the shots inside the arc and at the line and Dallas wins the game comfortably: 31 two-point makes to 22, a 10-point paint edge, five more made free throws, ten more rebounds. The Wings' two-point accuracy (31-of-48, 65%) was essentially level with Minnesota's (22-of-33, 67%), so this was not a case of one team simply shooting better. It is a case of two teams playing different games. Minnesota took 39 of its 72 field-goal attempts from three — 54% of its shots — against Dallas's 20 of 68, or 29%. When you launch 19 more threes and make them at 46%, the arithmetic of the three-point line does the rest: each make is worth 50% more than the two-point shots Dallas was winning, so Minnesota needed only to break even on volume to erase Dallas's interior edge, and it did far better than break even. The record itself is the mechanism, not a garnish. McBride's 10 makes accounted for 30 of Minnesota's 103 points and, on their own, more than covered the 13-point margin. Dallas earned more per shot it took. Minnesota took the shots that were worth more and got more of them off.
Room for Disagreement
This is one game, and the spine of it is a record — by definition the rarest kind of shooting night, not a repeatable edge. McBride's 10-of-14 is not a process Minnesota can bank on; regress her to even a strong 5-of-14 and Dallas likely wins mmodeled. A reasonable reading is simply "a shooter got hot and one team lost to variance," and the numbers do not refute it. The turnover gap complicates the tidy "Dallas was more efficient" framing: 18 giveaways to 11 means Dallas wasted roughly seven possessions worth about a point each, and several became Minnesota transition threes, so Dallas's per-shot efficiency overstates how well it actually used the ball [verified/modeled]. What is not in dispute is the closed arithmetic: Dallas won the non-three game by 23, Minnesota won the three-point game by 36, and 36 minus 23 is the final margin.

What a human would miss
Watch the highlights and you get a hot hand: a shooter hits 10 threes, sets a record, team wins. Read the whole sheet at once and the shape inverts. The team that lost was the more efficient one on nearly every conventional measure — 54% from the field, 65% on two-pointers, 91% from the line, a 10-point paint edge, ten more rebounds — and it outscored Minnesota by 23 points on every shot that wasn't a three. It still lost by 13, because the game was decided in the one column where it was buried: 18 made threes to 6, a 36-point swing that no amount of interior efficiency could answer.
