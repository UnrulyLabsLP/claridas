We read all 33 backs who carried 150+ times in 2025: the yards they made after contact ran 33% to 61% of their total

Pro Football Reference splits every rushing yard into what a back gained before a defender touched him and what he gained after. Read across the whole workhorse pool, the same rushing-yards line hides two very different stories — Jahmyr Gibbs got two-thirds of his yards before contact, Quinshon Judkins made 61% of his after it, on the thinnest before-contact cushion of the group.

Filed by the Claridas sports pod · August 23, 2026

The Facts
Every rushing yard divides cleanly in two: the ground a back covers before a defender touches him, and the ground he covers after. Pro Football Reference tracks both — yards before contact and yards after contact — and the two sum, exactly, to the rushing total. Across all 349 players in the 2025 advanced-rushing file, that identity holds in every row: before-contact plus after-contact equals total yards, no exceptions. We read all 33 running backs who carried the ball 150 or more times in the 2025 NFL season — the workhorse tier — and computed each one's after-contact share. It ran from 33.0% (Detroit's Jahmyr Gibbs) to 61.1% (Cleveland's Quinshon Judkins), a 28-point spread on a number that never appears in a box score. The mean was 46.5%, the median 46.2%. The gap widens at the point of contact itself. Gibbs gained 3.37 yards per carry before anyone touched him, the most of the 33; Judkins gained 1.40, the fewest — roughly 2.4 times as many before-contact yards per carry for one back as for the other. The three biggest rushers split differently from each other: James Cook led the group at 1,621 yards, 57.7% of it before contact; Derrick Henry ran for 1,595, 54.3% before contact; Jonathan Taylor's 1,585 came almost exactly half-and-half, 49.7% after. Six of the 33 sat below 40% after contact — McCaffrey, Barkley, Gibbs, D'Andre Swift, Kenneth Walker and rookie TreVeyon Henderson — and three above 55%: Ashton Jeanty, Judkins and Kimani Vidal.
The Analysis
The following is analysis, not fact. A fantasy draft board is built on totals — rushing yards, or the fantasy points that flow from them. The total is the one number every manager sees. What the whole-pool read surfaces is that the total is two numbers wearing the same jersey, and they don't come from the same place. Yards before contact lean on blocking, scheme and space; yards after contact lean on the back — his balance, his vision at the second level, the tackles he breaks — though the two are jointly produced, and the split is a lens, not a clean allocation of credit mmodeled. Gibbs and Judkins are the same statistic telling opposite stories. Gibbs's 1,223 yards arrived two-thirds before a defender arrived — the most before-contact room of the 33; Judkins's 827 came 61% after contact, on the thinnest before-contact cushion of any workhorse back in football. Jeanty, the rookie, made 57.0% of his yards after contact on 1.58 before-contact yards a carry — second-fewest of the 33 — while forcing 24 broken tackles. Taylor broke 27, the most in the group, and still landed at a 50/50 split, with 2.47 yards before contact to start. None of that changes a single player's yardage line. It only shows where the yards came from.
Room for Disagreement
The before/after split is a useful lens, not a clean partition of credit. Modeling by analyst Sam Hoppen finds yards before contact are shaped more by the back than the popular "it's the offensive line" reading assumes — of the variance the model attributes to on-field roles, roughly 60% traces to the runner and about 24% to the line, the rest to the quarterback — because vision and patience help a back reach open grass untouched. Those are shares of the role-attributable variance, not of all play-to-play variation in yards before contact, much of which the model leaves unexplained mmodeled. So "line-made" versus "back-made" is shorthand, not attribution. The levels also aren't portable between sources: PFF's contact tracking is charted differently from PFR's and produces different numbers for the same player, so the ranking here holds only within one provider's book mmodeled. The 150-carry cutoff is a choice; a lower bar would pull more committee backs into the tails. And this is one completed season — after-contact share carries year-to-year noise, and nothing here forecasts 2026 mmodeled. What is not in dispute is the arithmetic: across all 33 workhorse backs, the after-contact share of rushing yards ran from 33.0% to 61.1%.
The View From
Stare at one roster and a running back is a yardage total and a touchdown count. Read all 33 workhorse backs at once and a second axis appears underneath the first: how much of the ground each man covered came before a defender reached him, and how much after. Two backs can finish a season within a few yards of each other and have spent it doing almost opposite work.
