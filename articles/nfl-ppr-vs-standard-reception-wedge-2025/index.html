The gap between PPR and standard fantasy scoring is exactly one number — catches — and it reorders the receiver board

We read every 2025 pass-catcher on both scoring systems most leagues draft from. The two boards differ by a single input — receptions — and it sorts possession receivers and field-stretchers into opposite tiers.

Filed by the Claridas sports pod · August 20, 2026

The Facts
A single-team manager drafts from one ranking. There are two. In nflverse's complete 2025 regular-season file, all 2,020 players carry both a standard fantasy total and a points-per-reception (PPR) total, and for every one of them the second minus the first equals one stat: receptions. PPR does not sprinkle fractional credit that compounds through yards or touchdowns — it adds exactly one point per catch, so a player's entire "PPR premium" is his reception count, to the point. Puka Nacua's premium was 129, Trey McBride's 126, Ja'Marr Chase's 125, Christian McCaffrey's 102. Those two totals are the two boards fantasy leagues actually use. Rank all 241 wide receivers by each and the very top barely moves — the elite catch and score in both systems. The divergence lives in the startable middle. Among the top 48 WRs by PPR, 20 shift five or more spots once the catch point is removed; across the full 48 the median move is 3 and the maximum 12. The 14 who fall three or more places in standard scoring posted a median 78 catches and an 8.5-yard median average depth of target — possession receivers. The 13 who rise posted a median 55 catches and a 12.5-yard aDOT — field-stretchers. At the 36-deep cut, three names swap out of the startable tier and three swap in. Out: Keenan Allen (81 catches, 9.6 yards per reception), Jakobi Meyers (75, 11.1), Khalil Shakir (72, 10.0). In: Rome Odunze (44 catches, 15.0 yards per reception, 6 receiving touchdowns), Romeo Doubs (55, 13.2, 6), Tre Tucker (57, 12.2, 5). Same 2025 season, same box scores — the two scoring systems sort them into opposite tiers, and the sort key is catches. The running-back pool shows the identical signature in miniature: the two who drop out of the standard top-36, Rachaad White and Omarion Hampton, caught 40 and 32 balls; the two who climb in, Blake Corum and Tyler Allgeier, caught 8 and 14.
The Analysis
The following is analysis, not fact. The reordering is not noise sitting on top of a signal — it is the signal, isolated. Fantasy scoring usually mixes a repeatable input (snaps, routes, targets, catches) with a volatile one (touchdowns, long gains), and pulling them apart is the whole job. Here the arithmetic does it: the only thing standard and PPR disagree about is receptions, one of the most repeatable inputs a pass-catcher produces. So the choice of format is, mechanically, a choice about how much to pay for volume that already happened. Read the whole pool and the shape is clean. Wan'Dale Robinson finished the 2025 season as the WR14 in PPR on 217.9 points and 92 catches; strip the catch point and he is the WR23 on 125.9 — a different player, on the same routes. Rome Odunze did the reverse: fewer catches, deeper targets, more scores, and a tier he holds only when catches count for nothing. The single-team manager staring at one league's ranking never sees the other board, so the possession receiver's floor looks either paid-for or invisible depending on a setting chosen before the draft — and the same 78-catch season reads as a WR2 or a bench piece with no change to the underlying usage. What the format cannot do is manufacture the catches; it can only decide whether to score them.
Room for Disagreement
The effect is a middle-tier phenomenon, not a top-of-draft one. The elite receivers are format-proof — they win the volume and the efficiency columns at once — so the reordering is concentrated in the WR2/WR3 range, and even 48-deep only 20 of 48 move five or more spots. Half-PPR (0.5 per catch), now widely used, splits the identity in two: the premium becomes receptions divided by two, so the same reshuffle runs about half as far; this piece pulled the full-PPR and standard totals directly and the half-PPR figure is the interpolation, not a separate pull. Average depth of target and yards per reception describe the fallers and risers as a tendency, not a law — a handful of high-catch receivers also found the end zone often enough to hold their standard rank, and aDOT does not by itself decide any single case. And this is a 2025 record. It reports what the completed season did across the full pool; it makes no claim about 2026 usage, and it recommends no player, tier, or format.
The View From
From inside one league, the ranking looks like the ranking — a settled fact handed down before the draft. From the whole-pool read, it is one of two, and the toggle between them is a single column of the box score.
