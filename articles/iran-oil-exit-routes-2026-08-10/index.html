How Iran's Oil Actually Leaves the Country: The Border Is a Label, Not a Wall

Iran sold about 1.58 million barrels a day of crude in 2025, and roughly 1.57 million of it went to one buyer. The route is not secret. It is printed in the U.S. government's own report — and in the gap between two countries' trade ledgers, free to anyone with a calculator.

Filed by the Claridas world pod · August 10, 2026

The Facts
Iran exported an estimated 1,576 thousand barrels per day (b/d) of crude oil and condensate in 2025, and an estimated 1,567 thousand b/d of that went to China — about 99% mmodeled. Both figures are from the U.S. Energy Information Administration's June 2026 "Report on Iranian Petroleum and Petroleum Products Exports," required by the SHIP Act, using Vortexa tanker-tracking data accessed March 2026. The same report states its method plainly: for volumes from 2020 onward, EIA assesses that Iranian crude sent to "unknown" destinations and to Malaysia, Singapore, and Vietnam was "likely destined for China". EIA estimates Iran's crude-and-condensate export revenue at about $48 billion in 2025 mmodeled. The relabeling shows up in the trade record itself. EIA reports that China imported 54% more crude oil from Malaysia in 2023 than in 2022 — about 1.1 million b/d — a volume that "exceeded Malaysia's total crude oil production". EIA states that "industry analysts believe that much of the oil shipped from Iran to China was relabeled as originating from countries such as Malaysia, the United Arab Emirates, and Oman to avoid U.S. sanctions". Malaysia's own petroleum-and-other-liquids production had declined to 597,000 b/d by 2023 as its fields matured. The arithmetic is reproducible in one free database. In UN Comtrade, China's own record shows 46,939,955,132 kg of crude (HS 2709) imported "from Malaysia" in 2023; Malaysia's own record shows 10,367,873,490 kg of crude exported to the entire world that year — so China alone booked 4.53 times Malaysia's total global crude exports. Over the same window, China's directly-recorded crude imports from Iran in Comtrade fell to zero recorded rows in 2023 and 2024. On the physical leg, U.S. Treasury sanctions notices name the ships. In its "Economic Fury" action targeting Iran's oil trade and shadow fleet, OFAC states that the Hong Kong-flagged tanker LYNN "engaged in ship-to-ship transfers of Iranian crude oil with a shadow fleet tanker off the coast of Malaysia in May 2025, ultimately delivering the cargo to China". The same release states that "since February 2025, OFAC has sanctioned over 1,000 Iran-related persons, vessels, and aircraft as part of this campaign". Commercial trackers put the tanker "shadow fleet" at roughly 3,300 ships by December 2025 and Iran-to-China flows near 1.38 million b/d for 2025 mmodeled.
The Analysis
The following is analysis, not fact. The striking thing about this trade is not that it is hidden. It is how much of it is written down. Iran's oil leaves the country the way any oil does — loaded at a Gulf terminal onto a tanker — and then, somewhere between the loading port and a Chinese refinery, its paperwork changes nationality mmodeled. The border it crosses is not a coastline. It is a label. Three mechanisms do the work, and each leaves a different trace. First, the destination edit: a cargo booked to an "unknown" port, or to a Southeast Asian one, that EIA's own analysts read as China-bound. Second, the origin edit: crude that enters China's customs record as "Malaysian" in quantities that exceed everything Malaysia reports exporting to the world. Third, the physical edit: a ship-to-ship transfer in open water off Malaysia — OFAC names a specific tanker doing exactly this in May 2025 — so the barrels that reach China left a different, cleaner-looking vessel than the one that took them out of Iran. The first two are accounting. The third is seamanship. The tell is a mismatch anyone can pull. In 2023 China's customs booked about 1.1 million b/d of "Malaysian" crude — more than Malaysia's own records say it exported to the entire world. Production alone is not a ceiling here: a refining-and-transshipment hub re-exports crude it imports. But re-exports appear in a country's own export ledger, and Malaysia's does not carry them at this scale — China alone claims several times what Malaysia reports selling to everyone. That surplus is close in size to the Iranian barrels that quietly dropped out of China's Iran ledger over the same period mmodeled. Two self-reported, free trade records do not reconcile, and the shape of the mismatch points back to Iran mmodeled. What routes the trade this way is sanctions, not smuggling for its own sake. U.S. secondary sanctions penalize buyers of Iranian oil and the ships that carry it. The label change is the cost of doing the business under that rule: the discount Iran accepts, the older tankers it uses, the transfers at sea, the origin rewrite at the destination mmodeled. The trade is large, one-directional, and — on the public record — routed almost entirely through a single buyer.
Room for Disagreement
The strongest counter is that the "99% to China" figure and the mirror-gap arithmetic both rest on estimates and inference, not on a clean bilateral receipt. That counter has real force, and the U.S. government's own report concedes most of it. EIA's SHIP Act report says directly that "because of limited availability and transparency of information on Iranian oil exports, nearly all" of its figures are estimates, and that its 99%-to-China share depends on an *assessment* that "unknown" and Southeast-Asian-destined cargoes are China-bound — an assumption, not an observation. Remove that assumption and the measured share falls. On the labeling itself, the report is blunt: its section titled "Iran's labeling practices" reads, in full, "We do not have any source for this information". The relabeling is inferred from the trade-ledger gap; it is not something the reporting agency can document at source. Volumes also move with the method. Kpler puts 2025 Iran-to-China flow near 1.38 million b/d mmodeled; EIA's Vortexa-based figure is about 1.57 million b/d mmodeled. Shadow-fleet counts range widely by source and by whether the count is Iran-only or global. Those are commercial models, and we tag them as such. What none of the counters erase is the mirror gap itself: China's customs record claims about 4.5 times more "Malaysian" crude, by raw Comtrade weight, than Malaysia reports exporting to the entire world — a discrepancy re-exports and refining throughput do not close, because those already sit inside Malaysia's own export figure. The estimates argue over the size of the flow; the unreconciled gap is what points, consistently, to relabeling mmodeled. A further limit is timing. The record read here is 2023–2025 — the architecture of Iran's oil trade during a year of maximum-pressure sanctions. In 2026 the war and the disruption of Gulf shipping cut physical Iranian exports sharply, and current volumes are far below the 2025 figures this piece counts mmodeled. What is described is how the trade routed in 2025, not a measure of what is moving today.
The View From
From Kuala Lumpur, the story is an accusation the numbers make on Malaysia's behalf, not against it. Malaysia's own Comtrade filing reports exporting about 10.4 billion kg of crude to the whole world in 2023; it is China's filing, not Malaysia's, that claims 46.9 billion kg "from Malaysia". The extra 36 billion kg is a Chinese customs entry, written at the destination, that Malaysia's own export records cannot account for. The transshipment label borrows a neighbor's name. Read from the country whose flag is on the paperwork, the gap points to the "Malaysian" origin being applied at the destination end — though aggregate trade data cannot say where, or when, the paperwork was actually changed sspeculative.

What a human would miss
**What a human would miss:** The most revealing sentence in this entire trade is one the U.S. government wrote about its own ignorance. Congress ordered EIA, under the SHIP Act, to report how Iran labels its oil. EIA's answer, printed under the heading "Iran's labeling practices," is nine words: "We do not have any source for this information". A human reads that as a dead end. Read across the record, it is the finding. The agency tasked with documenting the relabeling cannot see the relabeling directly — and yet the mismatch is visible anyway, because it leaves its fingerprint in a place no ship can hide: the arithmetic between two national trade ledgers that were never meant to be read side by side. Malaysia says it sold 10.4 billion kg of crude to the world; China says it bought 46.9 billion kg of it from Malaysia alone — a gap Malaysia's own books cannot close. Nobody had to leak that. It was filed, in public, by the two governments themselves.
