American wholly owns three of the airlines that fly as American Eagle — in June its mainline canceled 2.6% of flights, Envoy 3.4%, and PSA 6.9%, the highest rate of any reporting carrier

We read all 607,577 flights the 12 carriers filed with the DOT for June 2026. The two of American's three wholly-owned regionals that reported June flights — Envoy and PSA — canceled far more than its mainline, 3.4% and 6.9% against the mainline's 2.6%, even though, when they flew, their arrivals were no less punctual. The operating carrier is shown at booking, as DOT requires — but not that it can move the odds your flight operates at all.

The Facts
A ticket that says "American" can be flown by its mainline or by a regional operating as American Eagle — and in June 2026, which one flew the leg lined up with sharply different cancellation rates. The U.S. Department of Transportation's on-time database for June 2026 — the most recent month posted (file dated 2026-08-12, retrieved 2026-08-22) — records 607,577 scheduled domestic flights by the 12 carriers that report to it: 10,019 canceled (1.65%), 2,363 diverted (0.39%), and an on-time arrival rate of 74.29%. Each row names the operating carrier, so the mainline brand and the regional flying under it are separable in the record. American Eagle service is flown by five regional airlines; American Airlines wholly owns three of them — Envoy Air, PSA Airlines and Piedmont Airlines — while Republic Airways and SkyWest fly the brand under contract without American owning them. Piedmont reported no flights in the June file, so across the carriers American both owns and reports — its mainline plus Envoy and PSA — the cancellation rate climbs with each step away from the mainline. American mainline canceled 2,281 of 86,464 flights (2.64%); Envoy 993 of 29,207 (3.40%); PSA 1,437 of 20,871 (6.89%). PSA's 6.89% was the highest cancellation rate of any of the 12 reporting carriers in June; the lowest were Allegiant (0.54%), United (0.62%) and Southwest (0.62%). PSA canceled at 2.6 times American's own mainline rate. The rates are reported per operator, not pooled into one "American" figure — combining a mainline with two regionals produces a number not comparable to any single carrier's rate. Three days — June 11, 12 and 19 — carried 36.7% of the month's cancellations, and every carrier spiked on them; PSA canceled 24.4% of its flights across those three days. With those three dates removed, the order was unchanged: American mainline 1.72%, Envoy 2.28%, PSA 4.89%. When the regionals did fly, their arrivals were not less punctual: Envoy posted a 78.7% on-time arrival rate and PSA 72.4%, both above American mainline's 69.9%; on average arrival delay Envoy ran 17.3 minutes against the mainline's 28.6, and PSA's 28.8 essentially matched it.
The Analysis
The operating carrier is disclosed at booking — DOT requires airlines to identify it clearly — but it is rarely the number a traveler weighs against price and schedule. A fare booked as American Eagle shows as "American," with "operated by PSA Airlines" or "operated by Envoy Air" alongside it; the DOT files the flight under that operator, which is why the whole-record read separates what the brand blends. A passenger comparing "American" to "Delta" on a route is, on the regional-flown legs, comparing different airlines than the brand names suggest. Why the regionals cancel more is not something this file settles. Regional carriers fly smaller aircraft on thinner routes into more weather-exposed and slot-constrained airports, and in a disruption a network protects its highest-value flying first — any of which could push regional cancellation rates above a mainline's for reasons that have nothing to do with the regional's own reliability sspeculative. The record cannot separate route, fleet and network triage from the operator, so it cannot say why the gap exists — only that, across June, it did. What the file shows cleanly is descriptive and operator-level: the carriers flying under the American brand canceled at rates from 2.64% for the mainline to 6.89% for PSA — though these are different route networks, not the same flights compared head to head.
Room for Disagreement
The strongest counter is that this compares operations, not the same flying. Regionals and mainlines serve different route networks — regionals concentrate on short-haul spokes into secondary airports — so a raw cancellation-rate gap partly measures where each flies, not how well. A like-for-like test would hold the route fixed, which one month's file supports only thinly. The cancellation code is also self-reported: carriers coded weather (code B) on 65.9% of June's cancellations, and the carrier itself assigns that label. And June 2026 is a single month with its own storm pattern, not a trend — three days moved a third of the total. What survives all of that is descriptive: across every flight the 12 carriers filed, the leg flown by American's mainline, Envoy or PSA was canceled 2.64%, 3.40% or 6.89% of the time, and off the storm days the order did not change.
The View From
From the seat that books the trip, the operating carrier is disclosed at the fare, as DOT requires — but it is rarely the detail a traveler weighs against price and schedule. DOT's consumer report folds each mainline's branded regionals into the parent's number, so "American" reads as one carrier; the flight-level file is where the operators come apart. What one month can show a traveler who cannot absorb a cancellation is where June's difference lived — in which airline actually flew the leg — not a forecast of any single future flight sspeculative.
